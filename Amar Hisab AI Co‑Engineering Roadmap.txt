## 1. Purpose and Scope

### 1.1 Why This Roadmap Exists
Amar Hisab is a living, evolving system built by a hybrid team of human engineers and an ensemble of AI co‑engineers. This roadmap is the **single source of truth** that governs how every AI system – whether it is Claude, GPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM, Kimi, or any future model – must think, collaborate, make decisions, and produce artefacts within this project.

No AI may contribute to Amar Hisab without first internalising the rules, patterns, and quality gates defined in this document. It transforms AI from a generic code generator into a disciplined, context‑aware member of the engineering team.

### 1.2 Who Must Follow This Roadmap
- All AI models assisting with code generation, refactoring, testing, documentation, or architectural design.
- Human engineers who prompt, review, and integrate AI‑generated output – they must know what to expect and how to enforce the rules.
- The CI/CD pipeline itself, when it invokes AI for automated review or remediation.

### 1.3 What This Roadmap Covers
- The **AI‑First Development Philosophy** and the division of labour between human and machine.
- **Context Management** – how AI obtains, maintains, and respects project knowledge.
- **Decision‑Making Protocols** – when AI may autonomously decide, when it must ask, and how it resolves ambiguity.
- **Architectural Consistency Enforcement** – the immutable rules that keep the codebase aligned with the Architecture Book.
- **Code Generation Standards** – templates, naming conventions, error‑handling patterns, and module structure that AI must follow.
- **Testing & Quality Gates** – what AI must test, how tests must be written, and the quality bar for generated code.
- **Documentation Evolution** – how AI maintains and updates the living documentation suite as the system evolves.
- **Security & Compliance** – rules that prevent AI from introducing vulnerabilities or violating data privacy.
- **Long‑Term Evolution** – how the roadmap itself is updated, and how AI adapts as the project grows.

## 2. The AI‑First Development Philosophy

Amar Hisab is built on the belief that **humans set the vision, make the hard trade‑offs, and own the accountability; AI amplifies execution**. The following principles are non‑negotiable.

### 2.1 Human Ownership, AI Execution
- **Humans are responsible** for architectural decisions, business logic verification, security review, and final sign‑off.
- **AI is responsible** for generating implementation‑ready code, tests, documentation drafts, and data migration scripts that strictly follow the established contracts.

### 2.2 Architecture as the Constitution
Every AI contribution must be traceable back to a section of the Architecture Book, Database Book, Proto Contract Book, or Event Catalog. If a requested change contradicts an architectural decision, the AI must **flag the conflict and refuse to proceed**, rather than silently generating non‑compliant code.

### 2.3 Context is Not an Afterthought
AI performance is directly proportional to the quality and completeness of the context it is given. The project will maintain a **Context Bundle** – a curated, versioned snapshot of the most relevant documentation that is fed to AI before any significant interaction. The AI itself must learn to request additional context when the bundle is insufficient.

### 2.4 Incremental, Verifiable Progress
Every AI‑generated unit (a function, a class, a module, a test suite) must be independently verifiable. The AI must produce output that can be reviewed in isolation, tested against its specification, and integrated without breaking existing behaviour.

### 2.5 The AI is a Teammate, Not an Oracle
AI contributions are proposals, not mandates. They must be clear, well‑commented, and humble in their assumptions. When the AI is uncertain, it must explicitly say so and present options, rather than inventing a plausible but incorrect solution.

## 3. Context Management Protocol

Every AI model contributing to Amar Hisab operates on a simple, brutal truth: **output quality is a direct function of input context quality**. This protocol defines how AI obtains, validates, maintains, and respects the project’s knowledge base before generating any artefact. It is the single most important operational discipline in this roadmap.

### 3.1 The Context Bundle

The Context Bundle is a curated, versioned snapshot of the minimum documentation required to make any meaningful contribution. It is maintained by the human engineering team and stored in the repository (`/docs/context-bundle/`). The bundle includes:

- `architecture-summary.md` – a 3‑page digest of the Architecture Book, focused on the layers, module boundaries, and key design decisions.
- `database-schema-summary.md` – the complete DDL for all active tables (auto‑generated from the Database Book).
- `api-contracts-summary.md` – the OpenAPI specification and gRPC service definitions (generated from the Proto Contract Book).
- `events-summary.md` – the list of all domain and sync events with their payloads.
- `coding-standards.md` – Dart/Flutter conventions, naming rules, and mandatory patterns (e.g., use of `freezed`, Riverpod, shelf middleware).
- `current-sprint-backlog.md` – the list of active user stories and tasks (optional, for sprint‑specific work).

For every significant AI interaction, the human engineer will provide the relevant portions of the Context Bundle as part of the prompt. The AI must not assume it has any context beyond what is explicitly given, unless the AI has persistent memory (e.g., a project‑specific Claude workspace or a custom GPT with knowledge files). In that case, the AI must still verify that its embedded knowledge matches the latest version of the bundle.

### 3.2 Context Freshness and Version Alignment

Documentation is living. The Context Bundle carries a version number (e.g., `v2.3.1`) that must be cited in every AI prompt. The AI is responsible for:

1. **Checking the context version** at the start of a conversation. If the version is older than the current release, the AI must request an update.
2. **Never inventing APIs or schemas** that are not present in the provided context. If a required entity is missing, the AI must ask for the relevant section of the Database Book or Proto Contract Book, rather than fabricating a compatible but incorrect structure.
3. **Flagging inconsistencies** between the prompt and the context. Example: “Your request adds a `discount_limit` field to the `sale` table, but the provided DDL does not include this column. Should I proceed with a schema migration, or is there an updated DDL?”

This discipline prevents AI from silently drifting the codebase away from the documented architecture.

### 3.3 The Minimum Viable Context Principle

An AI must never generate code for a module unless it has, at minimum, the following four items:

1. **The entity’s DDL** (or domain model definition).
2. **The API contract** (endpoint, request/response shape).
3. **The architectural layer** the code belongs to (domain, application, infrastructure, API).
4. **The expected dependencies** (other services, repositories, event bus).

If any of these is missing, the AI must halt generation and explicitly request the missing piece. For example: “I can draft the `SalesService`, but I need the interface for `InventoryService` to ensure correct stock deduction. Please provide the `InventoryService` abstract class or its method signatures.”

### 3.4 Context Overload and Prioritization

In long‑running conversations, context windows may fill. AI must proactively manage this by:

- **Requesting summaries** of earlier parts of the conversation when the human engineer is providing new context. “I’ve reached my context limit. Can you summarise the decisions made so far, and I will continue from there?”
- **Self‑pruning** – if given a large document, identifying and extracting only the parts relevant to the current task. The AI must state which sections it is using and which it is ignoring, so the human can verify.
- **Maintaining a working memory** within the conversation: key decisions, generated file names, and agreed‑upon patterns must be explicitly restated at the start of each major prompt to compensate for limited context windows.

### 3.5 The “Unlearn and Relearn” Rule

AI models trained on general codebases have implicit biases (e.g., using `sequelize` for Node.js, or assuming a cloud‑first REST API). For Amar Hisab, the AI must unlearn these defaults and adopt the project’s specific stack:

- **SQLite with `sqflite`**, not PostgreSQL or Firebase.
- **Dart `shelf`** for local HTTP, not Express or Django.
- **Flutter Riverpod** for state, not Redux.
- **Offline‑first transactional outbox**, not direct cloud writes.

The Context Bundle explicitly states these defaults. If the AI produces code that assumes a different stack, the human engineer will reject it, and the AI must correct itself.

### 3.6 Context Request Protocol

When the AI needs additional information, it must follow a structured request format to minimise back‑and‑forth:

```
[CONTEXT REQUEST]
- Module: Sales
- Missing Item: Abstract interface for InventoryService
- Why Needed: To inject dependency into SalesService and follow ports & adapters pattern.
- Recommended Source: Architecture Book Section 14.3, or existing code at `inventory/domain/services/inventory_service.dart`
```

This makes it easy for the human engineer to copy‑paste the exact needed snippet.

## 4. Decision-Making Protocol

AI co-engineers are not passive code generators; they must make thousands of micro-decisions during development—method signatures, error-handling strategies, transaction boundaries, naming conventions. This protocol defines **when AI may decide, when it must ask, and how it must resolve ambiguity** in a way that aligns with Amar Hisab’s architectural integrity.

### 4.1 Decision Autonomy Levels

Every decision an AI faces falls into one of four tiers. The tier determines whether the AI can proceed autonomously or must escalate to a human.

| Tier | Name | Description | Examples |
|------|------|-------------|----------|
| **Tier 0 – Fully Autonomous** | AI decides and implements without asking. | Formatting, import ordering, adding a missing `const` keyword, generating a getter for an existing field, writing a unit test that follows an existing pattern. |
| **Tier 1 – Autonomous with Notification** | AI decides and implements, but must explicitly state the decision in its response. | Choosing between `async`/`await` vs. returning a `Future` directly, selecting a specific error code from the standard set, deciding whether to use a `for` loop or a `.map()` on a small collection. |
| **Tier 2 – Escalation Required** | AI must present a **structured recommendation** with trade-offs and wait for human approval before writing code. | Adding a new dependency to `pubspec.yaml`, creating a new module boundary, deviating from the documented architecture, introducing a new database index that might affect write performance. |
| **Tier 3 – Forbidden** | AI must refuse and flag the architectural violation. | Changing the signature of a public API that has consumers, modifying the database schema without a migration script, bypassing the transactional outbox pattern, writing code that stores plaintext secrets. |

The AI must self-classify its decisions at the start of each response when a non‑trivial choice is made: *“[Decision: Tier 1 – I chose to use a switch expression rather than if‑else for readability. This does not change behavior.]”*

### 4.2 The Escalation Framework

When a decision reaches Tier 2, the AI must not simply state the problem; it must provide a **decision brief** that enables the human to decide quickly.

**Structure of a Decision Brief:**
```
[DECISION BRIEF – Tier 2]
- Context: Adding a `batch_reservation` table to support pre‑allocating stock before payment.
- Options:
  A) New table `batch_reservations` with a TTL column; reservations expire after 15 minutes.
  B) Use the existing `stock` table by adding a `reserved_quantity` column.
- Recommendation: Option A, because it keeps the `stock` table as the materialized truth and follows the event‑sourcing pattern.
- Impact: Option A requires a new migration and a background job to expire reservations. No existing APIs change.
- Question: Do you approve Option A, or should we explore further?
```

This format forces the AI to clarify its reasoning, exposes the trade-offs, and respects the human’s authority.

### 4.3 Resolving Ambiguity

Ambiguity is inevitable in natural‑language requirements. When the AI encounters an unclear instruction, it must **not guess silently**. It must follow a three‑step resolution ladder:

1. **Consult the Context Bundle:** If the ambiguity can be resolved by checking the Architecture Book, Database Book, or existing code patterns, the AI must do so and cite its source. *“The Architecture Book Section 8.7 specifies last‑write‑wins for conflict resolution. I will apply that here.”*

2. **Make an Educated Assumption (Tier 1):** If the context is silent but the project has a clear convention, the AI may assume the convention holds and declare its assumption. *“The prompt does not specify the error format. Since the project uses a standard `ApiError` envelope, I will use that.”*

3. **Escalate (Tier 2):** If no convention exists and the choice materially affects behavior or architecture, the AI must present a decision brief. *“I see two valid approaches to handle this deadlock scenario. Please choose…”*

The AI must never fabricate a business rule. If the prompt says “apply a discount,” and the project has a `DiscountCalculator` service, the AI must use it. If no such service exists, the AI must ask whether to create one or implement inline logic.

### 4.4 Architectural Conflict Handling

The most critical decisions arise when an instruction conflicts with the documented architecture. The AI is the guardian of the architecture; it must treat the Architecture Book as the constitution.

**Conflict Response Protocol:**
1. **Identify the exact conflict:** Cite the relevant section of the Architecture Book or Database Book.
2. **Explain the violation:** Describe why the requested change would break a design principle, introduce a security risk, or create technical debt.
3. **Propose an alternative** that satisfies the requirement while respecting the architecture.
4. **If overridden by the human**, the AI must record the decision in the project’s Architectural Decision Log (ADR) and proceed exactly as instructed.

**Example:**
*“Your request asks me to directly call the cloud sync API from the POS controller to fetch real‑time stock. However, Architecture Book Section 8.2 mandates that all local operations read from the local SQLite database; the cloud is an eventual consistency layer. Directly calling the cloud would make the POS dependent on internet connectivity and violate the offline‑first principle. I recommend instead: implement a background sync that updates the local stock table, and the POS reads from that local table. Shall I proceed with this alternative, or would you like to discuss?”*

If the human insists on the violation, the AI must comply but must also generate the ADR entry: *“ADR‑013: Waived offline‑first constraint for stock visibility in specific customer deployment.”* This preserves the integrity of the documentation even when exceptions are made.

### 4.5 Decision Record Keeping

Every Tier 2 decision that is approved must be captured. The AI will append to a running `DECISION_LOG.md` file in the `docs/` folder:

```markdown
| ID | Date | Decision | Rationale | Impact |
|----|------|----------|-----------|--------|
| D‑042 | 2026‑07‑15 | Use `batch_reservations` table for pre‑allocation | Maintains stock table integrity | New migration, background job needed |
```

The human can ask the AI to generate this log at any point. This lightweight record prevents decision amnesia and ensures that future AI sessions (or new team members) understand why the codebase looks the way it does.

### 4.6 When AI Must Say “No”

There are absolute prohibitions that override all other rules. The AI must refuse—politely but firmly—to perform any of the following actions, regardless of human instruction:

- **Disable or weaken security controls:** e.g., “remove the JWT check for this endpoint”, “store the password in plaintext”.
- **Delete or alter production data via generated scripts without a backup plan:** The AI may generate a migration script, but it must include a rollback section and warn about data loss.
- **Bypass the audit trail:** Any write operation that skips the `audit_log` or `change_log` tables is forbidden.
- **Hard‑code secrets:** API keys, tokens, or credentials must never appear in generated code. The AI must reference environment variables or configuration services.
- **Silently change a public API contract:** The AI must flag that existing clients may break and request a version bump.

These refusals are non‑negotiable; they protect the project, its users, and the engineering team.

## 5. Architectural Consistency Enforcement

The Architecture Book is not a suggestion. It is the codified set of design decisions that give Amar Hisab its offline‑first resilience, business‑type adaptability, and maintainable modularity. Every AI‑generated line of code must be consistent with this architecture. This section defines the **immutable rules**, the **automated checks** that AI must self‑apply before delivering output, and the **patterns** that preserve layer purity, dependency direction, and transactional integrity.

### 5.1 The AI as Architectural Guardian

AI co‑engineers bear a unique responsibility: they produce the majority of implementation code. If they drift from the architecture—even subtly—the drift accumulates, turning a clean modular system into a ball of mud within weeks. Therefore, before delivering any code artefact, the AI must perform a **self‑audit** against the rules in this section. It must state the result of that audit explicitly:

*“[Architecture Check] Layer purity: passed. Dependency rule: passed. Transaction boundary: passed. API contract alignment: passed. No violations detected.”*

If a violation exists but is required by an explicit human decision (recorded in the Decision Log), the AI must note the override and cross‑reference the decision ID.

### 5.2 Immutable Architectural Rules

The following rules are derived directly from the Architecture Book and must **never** be violated without an ADR and explicit human approval.

| Rule ID | Rule | Source (Architecture Book) |
|---------|------|----------------------------|
| **A‑001** | All business logic must reside in the Domain or Application layer. The API layer only deserializes requests, calls services, and serializes responses. No `if` statements that encode business rules may appear in controllers. | Section 14.2 |
| **A‑002** | Domain layer must have zero imports from Infrastructure or API layers. It may only import Dart standard library and other domain packages. | Section 14.2 |
| **A‑003** | Every module exposes its services through abstract interfaces (ports). Other modules depend only on the interface, never on the concrete implementation. | Section 14.3 |
| **A‑004** | All database writes must happen within a single SQLite transaction per logical unit of work. The transaction object (or a `TransactionContext`) must be passed explicitly through the service layer. No global transaction state. | Section 14.4 |
| **A‑005** | Every data mutation that must be synced must be recorded in the `change_log` table within the same transaction as the business data (transactional outbox). | Section 16.6 |
| **A‑006** | Once a `journal_entry` has `status = 'posted'`, it must never be updated or deleted. Corrections are made via reversing entries. | Section 13.5 |
| **A‑007** | The `stock` summary table is denormalised; the authoritative source of stock is the `stock_movements` table. Any code that reads stock for business decisions must derive it from movements, not rely solely on the summary table. | Section 13.6 |
| **A‑008** | No external API call (SMS, payment gateway, cloud sync) may be made from within a database transaction. All external calls must happen after the transaction commits, triggered by domain events. | Section 14.8, 16.2 |
| **A‑009** | API responses must use the standard envelope: `{ "success": true/false, "data": ..., "error": { "code": "...", "message": "..." } }`. No custom top‑level shapes. | Proto Contract Book Section 2.6 |
| **A‑010** | All monetary values must be represented as `REAL` in the database and rounded to two decimal places in the application layer before persistence. No floating‑point arithmetic without rounding. | Database Book Section 1.3 |

These ten rules are the minimum bar. The AI must memorize them and apply them to every piece of code it generates.

### 5.3 Layer Purity Checks

The AI must verify that the generated code respects the four‑layer structure (Interface, Application, Domain, Infrastructure) and that dependencies flow inward.

**Checklist (to be mentally executed before output):**
- Does any `domain/` file import `sqflite`, `shelf`, `dart:io`, or any infrastructure‑specific package? If yes → **violation**.
- Does any `application/` service directly manipulate HTTP requests or responses? If yes → **violation**.
- Does any `infrastructure/` repository implementation return domain entities, not raw `Map<String, dynamic>`? The mapping must happen inside the infrastructure layer. If raw data leaks → **violation**.
- Are all injected dependencies declared as abstract types (interfaces) in the constructor, not concrete classes? If a service imports and instantiates another service’s implementation → **violation** of the ports and adapters pattern.

### 5.4 Dependency Direction Enforcement

The dependency graph is:  
**Interface → Application → Domain ← Infrastructure**

This means:
- Application depends on Domain. Domain knows nothing of Application.
- Infrastructure depends on Domain (through repository interfaces). Domain knows nothing of Infrastructure.
- API (Interface) depends on Application. Application knows nothing of API.

The AI must visually trace its imports. Any import arrow that points outward from Domain is a red flag. Any circular dependency between modules is a critical defect that the AI must not introduce.

### 5.5 Naming and Structural Conventions

Inconsistency in naming erodes understandability. The AI must follow these conventions without exception:

| Element | Convention | Example |
|---------|------------|---------|
| Entity classes | PascalCase, singular noun | `Sale`, `Product`, `JournalEntry` |
| Value objects | PascalCase, descriptive | `InvoiceNumber`, `Money`, `BatchNumber` |
| Abstract repositories | PascalCase, `XxxRepository` | `SaleRepository` |
| Repository implementations | `XxxRepositoryImpl` | `SaleRepositoryImpl` |
| Services | `XxxService` | `SalesService`, `InventoryService` |
| HTTP controllers | `XxxController` or `XxxApi` | `SalesApi` |
| Event classes | Past‑tense verb + noun | `SaleCompleted`, `PaymentReceived` |
| Database tables | Lower‑case, plural | `sales`, `products`, `batches` |
| API endpoints | `/api/v1/{resource}` | `/api/v1/sales` |
| Flutter widgets | PascalCase, descriptive suffix | `PosScreen`, `ProductSearchBar` |

The AI must generate code that blends seamlessly with existing code. If given an existing file as reference, the AI must match the style precisely (brace placement, trailing commas, import grouping).

### 5.6 Transactional Boundary Rules

Amar Hisab relies on SQLite transactions to maintain data integrity. AI‑generated service code must:

- Open a transaction at the service method level (or receive a `Transaction` object from the caller).
- Pass the same transaction object to every repository write and to any called service that performs writes (e.g., `InventoryService`, `AccountingService`).
- Never commit or rollback the transaction outside the orchestrating service.
- Never perform an `await` on an external I/O operation (HTTP call, file write) while a transaction is open. Any such call must be deferred to after the transaction commits, using the event bus.

If the AI generates a service method that opens a transaction and then calls a method that opens its own nested transaction, it must recognise this as a bug (SQLite supports nested transactions via savepoints, but the pattern must be explicit, not accidental). The AI must request clarification if the intended transactional scope is ambiguous.

### 5.7 API Contract Alignment

Before generating a controller, the AI must cross‑reference the endpoint with the Proto Contract Book. It must ensure:

- The URL path, HTTP method, and request/response body match the specification exactly.
- All mandatory fields in the request are validated; missing fields return `400 Bad Request` with a descriptive error code.
- The response envelope uses the correct `success`, `data`, and `error` structure.
- Pagination parameters (`limit`, `cursor`) are supported for collection endpoints.
- The appropriate RBAC permission (e.g., `sale:create`) is declared and enforced via middleware.

If the Proto Contract Book specifies a field as optional, the AI must not make it required. If the specification is missing for a new endpoint, the AI must propose a contract following the existing patterns and request approval before implementing.

### 5.8 Automated Self‑Check Prompt (AI Internal)

To systematize these checks, every AI that generates code for Amar Hisab should execute the following mental checklist (or be prompted with it) before delivering output:

```
Before submitting this code, verify:
1. Layer: Which layer(s) does this code touch? Are the imports correct for that layer?  
2. Dependencies: Do I depend on interfaces, not concretions?  
3. Transaction: If I'm writing data, am I inside a transaction? Am I calling external services inside the transaction?  
4. Change Log: For every entity mutation, did I insert into `change_log`?  
5. API Envelope: If this is an endpoint, does it return the standard envelope?  
6. Naming: Do all new names follow the project conventions?  
7. Security: Did I check RBAC? Am I logging to `audit_log`? Am I storing any secret?  
8. Reference: Did I cite the section of the Architecture/DB/Proto Book that authorizes this design?  
```

The AI must include a brief note confirming which checks passed. This builds trust with the human reviewer and catches mistakes before they enter the codebase.

### 5.9 Integration with CI/CD (Future)

While the initial enforcement is AI self‑discipline and human code review, these rules are designed to be automated. The project will adopt tools like:

- **Dart custom lint rules** that flag imports violating layer boundaries.
- **ArchUnit‑style tests** (using `test` package) that assert dependencies between packages conform to the defined graph.
- **OpenAPI schema validation** in CI that verifies API responses against the contract.
- **`change_log` coverage test**: a database trigger or integration test that ensures every entity table has a corresponding entry in `change_log` after a write.

When these automated checks exist, the AI will be required to reference them: *“I ran `flutter analyze` and the custom layer lint rule; no violations.”* The human reviewer then focuses on business logic, not structural compliance.

## 6. Code Generation Standards

The AI must produce code that is not merely functional, but **production‑grade**: consistent, idiomatic, testable, and immediately integrable into the Amar Hisab codebase. This section defines the mandatory patterns, file templates, error‑handling paradigms, documentation requirements, and performance considerations that govern every generated artifact.

### 6.1 Mandatory File Structure

Every Dart file must follow a strict internal layout. This ensures that any engineer—human or AI—can immediately locate the purpose of a file.

```
// 1. Copyright and license (if applicable)
// 2. Library/part directives
// 3. Imports (sorted: dart, package, project, relative)
// 4. Type definitions (typedefs, enums)
// 5. Top-level constants
// 6. Class/interface declarations
// 7. Extension methods
```

Within a class, the member order is:
1. Static fields
2. Instance fields (private, then public)
3. Constructors (generative, then factory)
4. Public methods (grouped by interface implementation)
5. Private methods
6. Getters and setters

The AI must not interleave public and private methods, nor place fields after methods, unless a specific Flutter widget lifecycle demands it.

### 6.2 Layer‑Specific Templates

Every class generated must correspond to exactly one architectural layer, and must follow the template for that layer.

#### 6.2.1 Domain Entity (Freezed)

```dart
import 'package:freezed_annotation/freezed_annotation.dart';

part 'sale.freezed.dart';
part 'sale.g.dart';

@freezed
class Sale with _$Sale {
  const factory Sale({
    required int id,
    required String invoiceNumber,
    required DateTime saleDate,
    // ... all fields
  }) = _Sale;

  factory Sale.fromJson(Map<String, dynamic> json) => _$SaleFromJson(json);
}
```

**Rules:**
- All entities must use `@freezed` for immutability.
- Every entity must have a `fromJson` factory.
- No business logic in entities; they are pure data.
- The primary constructor must be the canonical form; no secondary constructors that bypass validation.

#### 6.2.2 Value Object

```dart
class InvoiceNumber {
  final String value;

  const InvoiceNumber._(this.value);

  factory InvoiceNumber(String value) {
    if (value.isEmpty) {
      throw ArgumentError('Invoice number cannot be empty');
    }
    return InvoiceNumber._(value);
  }

  @override
  bool operator ==(Object other) =>
      identical(this, other) || other is InvoiceNumber && value == other.value;

  @override
  int get hashCode => value.hashCode;
}
```

**Rules:**
- Value objects must be immutable.
- Validation in the factory constructor; invalid state must throw.
- Implement equality and hashCode.

#### 6.2.3 Repository Interface

```dart
abstract class SaleRepository {
  Future<Sale?> findById(int id);
  Future<List<Sale>> findAll({DateTime? from, DateTime? to, int? customerId});
  Future<void> save(Sale sale);
  Future<void> update(Sale sale);
}
```

**Rules:**
- Repository interfaces live in the domain layer.
- Methods must use domain entities, never database rows.
- No SQL or database‑specific types.

#### 6.2.4 Repository Implementation

```dart
class SaleRepositoryImpl implements SaleRepository {
  final Database _db;

  SaleRepositoryImpl(this._db);

  @override
  Future<Sale?> findById(int id) async {
    final rows = await _db.query('sales', where: 'id = ?', whereArgs: [id]);
    if (rows.isEmpty) return null;
    return SaleMapper.fromMap(rows.first);
  }
  // ...
}
```

**Rules:**
- Constructor injects `Database` (from sqflite).
- Must not expose raw SQL to the service layer.
- Use a dedicated `Mapper` class for row‑to‑entity conversion.

#### 6.2.5 Application Service

```dart
class SalesService {
  final SaleRepository _saleRepo;
  final InventoryService _inventoryService;
  final AccountingService _accountingService;

  SalesService(this._saleRepo, this._inventoryService, this._accountingService);

  Future<Sale> createSale(Sale sale) async {
    await _db.transaction((txn) async {
      // orchestrate domain actions
    });
    // publish domain events after commit
  }
}
```

**Rules:**
- All dependencies are abstract interfaces (port).
- Constructor injection only; no service locator.
- Transaction management is explicit; the service receives or opens the transaction.
- The service is stateless.


- Role‑based permission checks are performed via middleware.

### 6.3 Error Handling Paradigm

Every AI‑generated method must handle errors predictably.

**Service Layer:**
- Use domain‑specific exceptions: `InsufficientStockException`, `DuplicateInvoiceException`, `ValidationException`.
- Services must throw, not return error codes.
- Every exception must carry a machine‑readable `errorCode` and a human‑readable `message`.

**API Controller:**
- Catch all exceptions and map them to the appropriate HTTP status and error envelope.
- Unhandled exceptions become `500 Internal Server Error` and must be logged with a correlation ID.
- Never expose stack traces in production responses.

**Standard Error Mapping:**

| Exception | HTTP Status | Error Code |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| `InsufficientStockException` | 409 | `INSUFFICIENT_STOCK` |
| `DuplicateInvoiceException` | 409 | `DUPLICATE_INVOICE` |
| `ValidationException` | 422 | `VALIDATION_ERROR` |
| `UnauthorizedException` | 403 | `FORBIDDEN` |
| `NotFoundException` | 404 | `NOT_FOUND` |
| Any other | 500 | `INTERNAL_ERROR` |

### 6.4 Code Generation and Annotations

The AI must leverage build‑runner for boilerplate reduction and must never manually write code that a generator can produce.

- **`@freezed`** for all entities and value objects.
- **`@JsonSerializable()`** for DTOs and API request/response models.
- **`@riverpod`** for all Flutter providers (state management).
- **Custom generators** (if needed) for repository SQL queries.

The AI must include the necessary `part` directives and mention that `build_runner` must be executed after generation.

### 6.5 Documentation and Comments

Every generated file and public member must be documented.

**Class documentation:**
```dart
/// Manages the lifecycle of a sales transaction, from creation to payment
/// and return. Orchestrates Inventory and Accounting services within a
/// single atomic transaction.
///
/// Implements use cases: CreateSale, ProcessReturn, AddPayment.
///
/// See: Architecture Book Section 14.2, Event Catalog SaleCompleted.
class SalesService { ... }
```

**Method documentation:**
```dart
/// Completes a new sale by deducting stock via FIFO, recording the sale
/// and payments, posting the double-entry journal, and recording sync
/// events in the transactional outbox.
///
/// Throws [InsufficientStockException] if any item lacks sufficient quantity.
/// Throws [ValidationException] if the payment amounts exceed the grand total.
Future<Sale> createSale(Sale sale) async { ... }
```

**Inline comments:** Use sparingly, only to explain non‑obvious business rules. Do not comment what the code literally does; comment *why*.

### 6.6 Performance and Efficiency

- All database queries must be scoped with `WHERE business_id = ?`.
- Use batch inserts for multiple rows: `db.batch()` for items, payments, change_log entries.
- Leverage WAL mode; do not override SQLite pragmas unless necessary.
- Flutter UI: use `const` constructors wherever possible; use `ListView.builder` for long lists; avoid rebuilds by extracting widgets.
- Avoid synchronous I/O on the UI thread; all database and file operations must be `async`.

### 6.7 Testing Standards (Mandatory for Every Generated Unit)

Every generated service, repository, or value object must be accompanied by unit tests.

**Value Object Tests:**
- Happy path: valid input creates object.
- Edge cases: boundary values, invalid inputs throw.

**Service Tests:**
- Must mock all dependencies.
- Test success scenario (return correct entity, dependencies called with correct arguments).
- Test each failure mode (insufficient stock, duplicate invoice).
- Verify transactional integrity: no partial saves.

**Repository Tests:**
- Use an in‑memory SQLite database (`sqflite_common_ffi`).
- Test insert, find, update, delete operations.

The AI must generate the test file alongside the production code, with the naming convention `{file}_test.dart`.

## 7. Testing & Quality Gates

Testing is not a separate phase performed after development. For AI‑generated code, **every unit of production code must be delivered with its corresponding tests**. The AI is responsible for ensuring that its output is verifiably correct, architecturally compliant, and ready for human review. This section defines the comprehensive testing strategy, the mandatory test artefacts, and the quality gates that every AI contribution must pass before it is considered complete.

### 7.1 The AI’s Testing Responsibility

The AI does not write tests as an afterthought. It writes them as an **integral part of the development process**, following the principle: *if there are no tests, the code is not done.* The AI must:

- Generate unit tests for every domain service, value object, entity, repository, and utility function.
- Generate widget tests for every UI component.
- Generate integration tests for every module’s service layer against a real database.
- Verify that its code passes all existing tests before proposing integration.
- Self‑assess the adequacy of its test coverage and explicitly state any gaps.

The human reviewer will reject code that lacks tests or contains tests that are obviously trivial or non‑validating.

### 7.2 The Amar Hisab Testing Pyramid

The project follows a layered testing strategy aligned with the Testing Pyramid, adapted for an offline‑first mobile/desktop application.

| Layer | Technology | Speed | Scope | Responsibility |
|-------|------------|-------|-------|----------------|
| **Unit Tests** | `flutter_test`, `mockito` | Very fast (< 1 s total per file) | Pure business logic, value objects, domain services | AI must produce for every service and entity |
| **Widget Tests** | `flutter_test` | Fast (headless rendering) | Individual Flutter widgets in isolation | AI must produce for every custom widget |
| **Integration Tests** | `sqflite_common_ffi`, `test` | Moderate (real SQLite I/O) | Service orchestration, repository, database | AI must produce for every critical workflow |
| **End‑to‑End Tests** | `integration_test` package, real devices | Slow (minutes) | Full user journeys, optionally with cloud sync | Human‑authored; AI may generate initial skeletons |
| **Architectural Tests** | Custom Dart tests, `analyzer` | Fast | Layer purity, dependency direction, API contract | AI must pass before PR |

### 7.3 Unit Test Standards

**7.3.1 Coverage Mandate**
Every domain service, value object, entity, repository interface (tested via its implementation), and utility function must have unit tests achieving **100% line and branch coverage**. This is achievable because domain logic is pure and free of I/O.

**7.3.2 Structure of a Unit Test**
- Use the `test` package and `group` for logical grouping.
- Name tests descriptively: `should deduct stock using FIFO and return COGS`.
- Follow the Arrange‑Act‑Assert pattern.
- All dependencies must be mocked using `mockito` or manual stub implementations.
- No real database or network calls.

**Example (Service Unit Test):**

```dart
void main() {
  late SalesService salesService;
  late MockSaleRepository saleRepo;
  late MockInventoryService inventoryService;
  late MockAccountingService accountingService;

  setUp(() {
    saleRepo = MockSaleRepository();
    inventoryService = MockInventoryService();
    accountingService = MockAccountingService();
    salesService = SalesService(saleRepo, inventoryService, accountingService);
  });

  group('createSale', () {
    test('should deduct stock, save sale, post journal, and return sale', () async {
      // Arrange
      final sale = Sale(/* ... */);
      when(inventoryService.deductStock(any, any, any)).thenAnswer((_) async => 100.0);
      when(saleRepo.save(any)).thenAnswer((_) async => 1);

      // Act
      final result = await salesService.createSale(sale);

      // Assert
      expect(result.id, 1);
      verify(inventoryService.deductStock(any, any, any)).called(1);
      verify(accountingService.postSale(any, any, any)).called(1);
    });

    test('should throw InsufficientStockException when inventory fails', () async {
      when(inventoryService.deductStock(any, any, any))
          .thenThrow(InsufficientStockException(101, 5, 3));
      expect(() => salesService.createSale(sale), throwsA(isA<InsufficientStockException>()));
    });
  });
}
```

**7.3.3 AI Self‑Check for Unit Tests**
Before delivering unit tests, the AI must ask:
- Do I test the happy path and all documented failure modes?
- Are all dependencies mocked, and are the mocks verifying the correct interactions?
- Do I verify state changes (return values) *and* side effects (calls to dependencies)?

### 7.4 Widget Test Standards

Flutter widgets must be tested to ensure they render correctly and respond to user interactions.

**7.4.1 Scope**
- Test a single widget in isolation; wrap it with necessary providers or mock data.
- Verify that UI elements are displayed, lists have expected items, buttons trigger callbacks.

**7.4.2 Example:**

```dart
testWidgets('should display product name and price in cart', (tester) async {
  final item = SaleItem(productId: 1, productName: 'Napa Extra', quantity: 2, unitPrice: 50.0);
  await tester.pumpWidget(MaterialApp(home: CartItemTile(item: item, onUpdate: (_) {}, onRemove: () {})));
  expect(find.text('Napa Extra'), findsOneWidget);
  expect(find.text('50.00'), findsOneWidget);
});
```

**7.4.3 Rules**
- Widget tests must not depend on live servers or databases; use mock data.
- Use `pumpWidget` to render, `pump` to refresh after state change, `tap` for interactions.

### 7.5 Integration Test Standards

Integration tests verify that the service layer works correctly with a real SQLite database, ensuring that SQL queries, transactions, and the transactional outbox pattern function as expected.

**7.5.1 Technology**
- Use `sqflite_common_ffi` to run a native SQLite in‑memory database on the desktop (no emulator required).
- Each test suite creates a fresh database, runs migrations, and tears it down.
- No mocking of the database; repositories use the real SQLite engine.

**7.5.2 Scope**
- Test complete workflows: e.g., create product → create purchase → create sale → verify stock and COGS.
- Verify that the `change_log` contains the correct entries after each transaction.
- Test concurrent access patterns if applicable (e.g., simultaneous sale and adjustment on same product).

**7.5.3 Example (Integration Test):**

```dart
void main() {
  late Database db;
  late SalesService salesService;
  late InventoryService inventoryService;
  late AccountingService accountingService;

  setUp(() async {
    sqfliteFfiInit();
    db = await databaseFactoryFfi.openDatabase(inMemoryDatabasePath);
    await DatabaseHelper(db).runMigrations();
    // wire up real repositories and services
  });

  test('full sale workflow should deduct stock and record change_log', () async {
    // Insert a product, a batch, stock
    // Create a sale
    final sale = Sale(/* ... */);
    await salesService.createSale(sale);

    // Verify stock deducted
    final stockAfter = await db.query('stock', where: 'product_id = ?', whereArgs: [101]);
    expect(stockAfter.first['quantity'], equals(expectedQty));

    // Verify change_log entries exist
    final logs = await db.query('change_log', where: 'entity_type = ?', whereArgs: ['sale']);
    expect(logs.length, greaterThan(0));
  });
}
```

**7.5.4 AI Responsibility**
The AI must generate integration tests for every service method that orchestrates multiple repositories or external services. This ensures that the transactional boundaries and outbox pattern are not violated.

### 7.6 End‑to‑End Test Guidelines (Human‑Augmented)

Full end‑to‑end tests (real devices, full stack) are expensive and are authored primarily by human QA engineers. However, the AI may generate **initial skeletons** that the human can refine.

- Use Flutter’s `integration_test` package.
- Cover the five critical business journeys:
  1. POS sale with cash payment and receipt printing.
  2. Purchase with batch tracking and stock update.
  3. Stock transfer between warehouses.
  4. Student enrollment, fee payment, and QR self‑service inquiry.
  5. Cloud sync between two simulated devices (if cloud sync is active).

The AI‑generated skeleton must include the test structure, the expected screen flow, and the assertions, but may leave device‑specific interactions (e.g., physical scanner) to be filled in by a human.

### 7.7 Architectural Conformance Tests (Automated)

A suite of lightweight tests will verify that the codebase adheres to the architectural rules defined in Section 5. These tests are not about business logic; they are about structure.

**7.7.1 Package Dependency Tests**
Using Dart’s `analyzer` or a custom test that parses `import` statements, verify:
- Domain packages never import Infrastructure or API packages.
- Module A does not import Module B’s concrete implementation.

**7.7.2 Layer Integrity Tests**
- Scan all Dart files: no `shelf` imports in `domain/` or `application/`.
- No `sqflite` imports in `application/` (repositories abstract the database).
- No `dart:io` in domain (except for logging utilities, explicitly allowed).

**7.7.3 API Contract Tests**
- Parse the OpenAPI spec and validate that every registered shelf route matches the spec’s path and method.
- Use a linter or custom script that can be run in CI.

The AI must be aware of these tests and ensure that any new code it generates will pass them. It can also propose new architectural tests when it identifies a recurring pattern that should be enforced.

### 7.8 Performance Tests (Targeted)

The AI must not introduce performance regressions. For critical paths, the AI should provide basic performance assertions:

- **FIFO deduction:** on a dataset of 10,000 batches, the deduction should complete in under 50 ms. The test inserts synthetic data and measures execution time.
- **Sale save:** the entire service call (including inventory and accounting) should complete in under 200 ms in integration tests on reference hardware.
- **Report generation:** a monthly sales report on 10,000 transactions should generate in under 2 seconds.

These tests are not part of every commit but are run in the nightly pipeline. The AI must use `Stopwatch` in test code to verify.

### 7.9 Coverage Requirements and Reporting

- **Unit tests:** 100% line and branch coverage for all domain and application service classes.
- **Widget tests:** All custom widgets must have at least one smoke test that verifies they render without error.
- **Integration tests:** All repository implementations and service orchestration methods must be covered by at least one integration test.

The AI must generate a simple coverage report for its own code: *“Coverage summary: SaleService 100%, SaleRepository 100%, Sale entity 100%.”* The human reviewer will confirm this with the actual coverage tool (e.g., `flutter test --coverage`).

### 7.10 AI Quality Gate Checklist (Self‑Assessment)

Before the AI delivers any batch of code, it must append a quality gate summary:

```
[QUALITY GATE]
- Unit tests: 15/15 passing, coverage 100% for all new files
- Widget tests: 2/2 passing for new POS widgets
- Integration tests: 1/1 passing for SalesService
- Architectural conformance: no layer violations detected
- API contract: new endpoint matches OpenAPI spec
- Performance: FIFO deduction benchmark passed (23 ms)
- Code style: dart format & dart analyze clean
- All existing tests still passing
```

If any gate fails, the AI must fix the issue before delivering, or flag it explicitly with a reason and a proposed remediation plan.

### 7.11 Integration with CI/CD Pipeline

The human team will configure the CI pipeline (GitHub Actions / GitLab CI) to automatically:

1. Run `flutter analyze` and `dart format --check`.
2. Run unit and widget tests.
3. Run integration tests (using `sqflite_common_ffi`).
4. Run architectural conformance tests.
5. Generate a coverage report and compare against the previous baseline.

The AI’s role is to ensure that the code it delivers will pass this pipeline. It cannot rely on the human to fix test failures. If the AI anticipates a potential integration issue (e.g., a new dependency that might not be available on CI), it must highlight this in its delivery notes.

### 7.12 Test Data and Fixtures

AI‑generated tests must use deterministic, self‑contained test data. They must not rely on pre‑existing database state or external services. Use factory methods (possibly AI‑generated) to create valid entity instances for testing. Example: `SaleTestFactory.createValidSale()`.

The AI must also generate any necessary SQL seed scripts for integration tests, and clean up after tests (transactions are rolled back; no permanent data remains).

## 8. Documentation Evolution and Maintenance

Documentation is not a static artefact produced once and forgotten. In Amar Hisab, the living documentation suite—Architecture Book, Database Book, Proto Contract Book, Event Catalog, RBAC Book, Training Manual, and the AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap itself—evolves in lockstep with the codebase. The AI is both a consumer and a producer of this documentation. This section defines how AI must maintain, update, and validate documentation so that it remains the single source of truth throughout the project’s lifetime.

### 8.1 The Principle of Code‑Documentation Co‑Evolution

Every change to the codebase has a corresponding documentation impact. The AI must treat documentation updates as a mandatory part of any implementation task, not an optional extra. When the AI generates code that:

- Introduces a new entity, table, or API endpoint,
- Modifies an existing database schema,
- Changes the signature of a public API,
- Alters a business rule or domain event,
- Adds or removes a module or feature,

the AI must also propose the corresponding updates to the relevant documentation files. If the human does not explicitly request documentation updates, the AI must still flag them as required and offer to produce them.

### 8.2 Mapping Code Changes to Documentation Impact

The AI must use a structured impact‑mapping table to determine which documentation files require modification for a given code change. This table is part of the AI’s internal knowledge base and must be applied automatically.

| Code Change | Documentation File(s) Impacted |
|-------------|-------------------------------|
| New table, column, index, or migration | Database Book |
| New REST endpoint or gRPC method | Proto Contract Book |
| Changed request/response shape | Proto Contract Book |
| New or modified domain event | Event Catalog |
| New role or permission change | RBAC Book |
| New user‑facing feature or screen | Training Manual, Sitemap, UX Design Guide |
| Significant architectural decision (e.g., new module, change in sync strategy) | Architecture Book, ADR Log |
| New external integration or plugin | Architecture Book (Integration section), Operations Runbook |
| Changes to deployment, backup, or security procedures | Production Deployment Guide, Operations Runbook |

Before finalizing any code delivery, the AI must run through this table and list the documentation impacts in its delivery notes. Example:

```
[DOCUMENTATION IMPACT]
- Database Book: new table `batch_reservations` added; DDL section needs update.
- Proto Contract Book: new endpoint `POST /api/v1/inventory/reserve` added.
- Event Catalog: new domain event `StockReserved` added.
- Architecture Book: Section 13.6 (Inventory) updated with reservation semantics.
- Training Manual: not impacted.
```

If the AI is uncertain about the full scope, it must ask the human to confirm the impact before proceeding.

### 8.3 How AI Updates Documentation

When AI produces documentation updates, it must follow these rules to maintain consistency and quality.

**8.3.1 Retrieve the Current Version**
The AI must never assume it knows the current state of a document. It must request the exact section or full document it is about to modify. If the document is large, the AI may ask for a specific section by heading. The human provides the Markdown snippet, and the AI then produces a revised version.

**8.3.2 Produce a Diff, Not a Regurgitation**
For existing documents, the AI must produce a clear diff: the exact text to be inserted, modified, or deleted, with surrounding context lines so the human can locate the change easily. For small changes, inline markdown with before/after is acceptable. For large structural re‑organisations, the AI should present a high‑level plan first, then produce the detailed text once approved.

**8.3.3 Preserve Voice and Formatting**
The AI must mimic the writing style of the existing document—formal, technical, precise, using consistent terminology. It must preserve the heading hierarchy, table formats, and code‑block conventions. If the document uses a particular pattern for version numbers, author fields, or cross‑references, the AI must follow it exactly.

**8.3.4 Cross‑Reference Integrity**
Documentation files are interlinked. When the AI updates one document, it must check for broken cross‑references in other documents. For example, if a table name changes in the Database Book, the Proto Contract Book and Event Catalog may also need updates. The AI must flag any cross‑reference impact, even if it does not have the full context to fix it immediately.

**8.3.5 Version Marking**
Every documentation file carries a version and last‑updated date. The AI must increment the minor version (or advise the human on the appropriate version bump) and update the date. If a change is backwards‑incompatible (e.g., removing an API endpoint), the AI must flag that a major version change may be required and consult the human.

### 8.4 Automated Documentation Generation

Where possible, documentation should be generated from code or structured data to eliminate manual drift. The AI must prioritise generating code that is a source of truth, and then generating documentation from that source.

**8.4.1 Database Schema Documentation**
The definitive DDL is stored in migration files. The AI can generate a script that produces the Database Book’s schema section automatically from the SQL migration files. The AI must be able to run this script and update the documentation accordingly, or instruct the human on how to do so.

**8.4.2 API Contract Documentation**
The OpenAPI specification (or proto files) is the source of truth for the REST API. The Proto Contract Book’s endpoint reference should be generated from the OpenAPI spec using a tool. The AI must ensure that any code change is reflected in the spec first, and then the documentation can be regenerated. The AI may generate a brief human‑readable summary from the spec.

**8.4.3 Event Catalog**
The event payloads can be inferred from the actual Dart classes (if structured consistently with `@freezed`). The AI can propose a script that scans event class definitions and outputs the Event Catalog’s payload tables. This keeps the catalog in sync with the code.

**8.4.4 Sitemap**
The sitemap can be partially derived from the Flutter route definitions. The AI can generate a skeleton sitemap and then enrich it with human‑friendly names.

The AI must advocate for automation wherever manual documentation maintenance becomes a bottleneck, and it must help design the generation scripts when asked.

### 8.5 Reviewing Human‑Produced Documentation

The AI is not only a producer; it also serves as a reviewer. When a human writes or updates documentation, the AI can be asked to review it for:

- **Consistency with the codebase:** Does the described API match the actual implementation? Are the table names correct?
- **Completeness:** Are all mandatory sections filled? Are there placeholders?
- **Clarity:** Is the language accessible to the target audience? Are acronyms defined?
- **Cross‑references:** Do links to other documents work?

The AI must provide constructive, specific feedback, citing the exact source in the codebase or architecture that contradicts or supports the documentation text.

### 8.6 The Documentation Audit Cycle

On a regular schedule (e.g., at the end of each sprint, or before a major release), the AI and human team conduct a documentation audit. The AI’s role in this audit is to:

1. **Inventory all documentation** and check the last‑updated date against the latest code commit.
2. **Flag stale documents** that have not been updated despite significant code changes in their domain.
3. **Generate a drift report:** a list of discrepancies between the documented architecture and the actual codebase (e.g., a service described in the Architecture Book that no longer exists, or a database column that is used but not documented).
4. **Propose a remediation plan:** a prioritised list of documentation updates needed to close the gaps.

The AI must produce this audit report in a structured format that the product owner and engineering lead can review and assign.

### 8.7 AI Self‑Documentation

The AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap itself is documentation that the AI must help maintain. When the project evolves to a point where the AI collaboration model needs refinement (e.g., a new generation of AI models becomes available, or the team adopts a new tool), the AI must propose updates to this roadmap. Changes to the roadmap must follow the Tier‑2 decision protocol: the AI presents a decision brief with the proposed changes, and the human approves before implementation.

### 8.8 Summary of Documentation Responsibilities

| Role | Responsibility |
|------|----------------|
| **Human** | Owns documentation strategy, approves major structural changes, writes high‑level vision documents, resolves conflicts. |
| **AI** | Detects documentation impact from code changes, generates documentation diffs, automates generation where possible, reviews human drafts, conducts audit cycles, maintains this roadmap. |

This division ensures that documentation remains accurate, comprehensive, and tightly coupled to the codebase, while freeing human engineers to focus on the most complex and strategic writing tasks.

## 9. Security & Compliance Envelope

Amar Hisab processes financial transactions, sensitive customer data, and operational records for thousands of businesses. A single security flaw introduced by an AI co‑engineer could erode that trust irreparably. This section establishes the **absolute prohibitions**, the **mandatory secure‑coding rules**, and the **data privacy principles** that every AI contribution must obey. No AI may generate code that violates this envelope, regardless of any human instruction to the contrary.

### 9.1 Absolute Prohibitions

These are the red lines. AI must refuse to generate, suggest, or endorse any code that performs these actions. Refusal must cite the relevant rule ID, explain the risk, and offer a secure alternative.

| Rule ID | Prohibition | Explanation |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| **S‑001** | Hard‑code secrets, API keys, tokens, or passwords in source code, configuration files, or database migrations. | Secrets must be injected via environment variables, a secure vault, or an encrypted settings table. AI must refuse to output any string that looks like a credential. |
| **S‑002** | Store plain‑text passwords or sensitive data. | Passwords must be hashed with Argon2id or bcrypt before storage. PII must be encrypted at rest using AES‑256. |
| **S‑003** | Disable or weaken authentication or authorisation checks. | No endpoint may bypass JWT validation or RBAC middleware, even temporarily. “Debug mode” must never disable security checks. |
| **S‑004** | Use dynamic SQL string concatenation or interpolation with user‑supplied input. | All database queries must use parameterised queries (`whereArgs`). Raw SQL with string interpolation is forbidden. |
| **S‑005** | Expose stack traces, internal error details, or database schemas in API error responses. | Production error responses must contain only the standard error envelope with a generic message and a correlation ID. |
| **S‑006** | Generate code that deletes or alters production data without a verified backup and rollback plan. | Migration scripts must include a `DOWN` section. Data‑destructive operations must require explicit human confirmation. |
| **S‑007** | Bypass the `audit_log` or `change_log` tables for any data mutation. | Every write to a business entity must be accompanied by an audit record. No exception. |
| **S‑008** | Transmit sensitive data over unencrypted channels outside the local network. | All cloud communication must use TLS 1.3. Public portal pages must use HTTPS (local network HTTP is an accepted exception per Architecture Book 10.4.3). |
| **S‑009** | Use outdated or vulnerable cryptographic algorithms (MD5, SHA‑1, DES, RC4). | The AI must verify that any cryptographic operation uses approved algorithms (AES‑256‑GCM, Argon2id, Ed25519). |

When the AI refuses under S‑001 through S‑009, it must state: *“I cannot generate this code because it violates S‑00X: [rule]. Here is a secure alternative: [alternative].”*

### 9.2 Mandatory Secure‑Coding Rules

Every AI‑generated file must comply with the following rules. The AI is responsible for self‑auditing its code against each rule.

**9.2.1 Input Validation**
- Every API request body must be validated against a schema (using a validation library or manual checks in the controller).
- String lengths, numeric ranges, and enum values must be enforced before any business logic runs.
- Validation errors must return `400 Bad Request` with a descriptive `error.code` and `error.message`.
- SQL injection is prevented by using parameterised queries exclusively. The AI must never use raw string interpolation for database queries.

**9.2.2 Output Encoding**
- JSON responses are automatically safe due to the `jsonEncode` function. However, if the AI generates HTML or JavaScript for the Public Portal, it must apply context‑appropriate output encoding to prevent XSS.

**9.2.3 Authentication & Session Management**
- All internal API endpoints (under `/api/v1/`) must be protected by JWT validation middleware. The JWT must have an expiration of 8 hours and be signed with a unique secret per installation.
- The Public Portal API uses portal tokens or student ID + secret. The AI must not expose an internal endpoint under the public path.
- Password reset flows must generate time‑limited, single‑use tokens.

**9.2.4 Authorisation (RBAC)**
- Every API endpoint must declare its required permission (e.g., `sale:create`). The shelf middleware enforces this.
- In the service layer, additional fine‑grained checks (e.g., cashier cannot apply more than 10% discount) must be implemented based on the user’s role, which is extracted from the JWT claims.
- The AI must never rely on client‑side UI hiding as a security measure; the server is the final enforcer.

**9.2.5 Sensitive Data Handling**
- Customer phone numbers, student secrets, and personally identifiable information (PII) must be stored encrypted if database encryption is enabled, and must not be logged at INFO level or above.
- The AI must redact sensitive fields when generating log statements. Use a structured logger with redaction.
- Export functions (Excel, Google Sheets) must warn the user if they contain PII and offer an anonymised option.

**9.2.6 Dependency Security**
- When adding a new package to `pubspec.yaml`, the AI must check its publish date, popularity, and known vulnerabilities (to the extent of its knowledge). It must prefer well‑maintained packages with compatible licenses.
- The AI must not suggest a package with a restrictive license (e.g., GPL) for the core product. MIT, BSD, Apache 2.0 are preferred.

### 9.3 Audit Trail Integrity

The `audit_log` table is the immutable record of all data changes. The AI must ensure:

- Every `INSERT`, `UPDATE`, and `DELETE` on a tracked entity writes a corresponding row to `audit_log`, capturing the user ID, entity type, entity ID, field‑level old/new values, and a timestamp.
- The `audit_log` table is append‑only at the application level. The AI must never generate code that updates or deletes audit records.
- For financial entities (`journal_entries`, `journal_lines`), posted entries must be immutable. Edits must be prevented by the application and backed by database triggers.

The AI must include audit trail verification in its integration tests: after any mutating operation, assert that an audit record exists with the correct user and action.

### 9.4 Data Privacy and Lifecycle

Amar Hisab businesses may collect customer and student data. The AI must design with privacy in mind:

- **Data Minimisation:** Only fields explicitly defined in the Database Book should be generated. The AI must not add unnecessary PII fields.
- **Anonymisation:** The AI must generate an anonymisation script (or service method) that replaces PII with a generic identifier while preserving transactional data for accounting. This is used when a customer requests deletion.
- **Data Export:** Users can export their complete data at any time. The AI must ensure export functions are secure (accessible only to Owner) and complete.
- **Retention:** The AI must respect the data retention policies defined in the Database Book (e.g., audit logs archived after 2 years). It may generate scheduled archival jobs.

### 9.5 Cloud Sync Security

When the AI generates code related to the optional cloud sync service, it must enforce:

- All communication over TLS 1.3.
- Device authentication via mTLS or device‑specific JWT.
- End‑to‑end encryption of sync payloads (the cloud stores encrypted blobs; decryption keys reside only on the local devices).
- The cloud service must never have access to plain‑text business data. The local server encrypts change payloads before sending.

The AI must refuse any design that sends unencrypted business data to the cloud, even if requested by a human who does not fully understand the privacy implications.

### 9.6 Security Self‑Audit Checklist

Before delivering any code, the AI must append a security gate summary:

```
[SECURITY GATE]
- Secrets check: No hard‑coded keys, tokens, or passwords found.
- SQL injection: All queries use parameterised statements. No string concatenation.
- Input validation: All API inputs validated; invalid input returns 400.
- Authentication: Endpoint protected by JWT middleware; permission required: sale:create.
- Audit trail: Every mutating operation writes to `audit_log`. Verified in integration test.
- PII: No sensitive data logged at INFO level or above; export functions offer anonymisation.
- Dependencies: New package `package_name` is MIT‑licensed, well‑maintained, no known vulnerabilities.
- TLS: Cloud communication uses TLS 1.3; local communication is HTTP (allowed per Architecture Book).
- Encryption: Passwords hashed with Argon2id; cloud sync payloads encrypted end‑to‑end.
- Prohibitions: No violations of S‑001 through S‑009 detected.
```

If any check fails, the AI must resolve the issue before delivering, or flag it as a Tier‑2 decision with a clear risk explanation and a secure alternative.

### 9.7 Incident Response Readiness

The AI may be called upon to assist in a security incident (e.g., a vulnerability is discovered in a library). The AI must:

- Quickly identify all files and modules affected by the vulnerable dependency.
- Propose a remediation plan: update the library, or if not possible, suggest a workaround or alternative library.
- Generate a summary for the incident report, including the scope of impact and the fix applied.

The AI must never attempt to exploit a vulnerability or generate proof‑of‑concept attack code within the project context. It may discuss vulnerabilities in abstract, educational terms, but not as executable code against the Amar Hisab system.

### 9.8 Compliance with Local Regulations

While the core development is in Bangladesh (and potentially expanding to India, Nepal), the AI must be aware of broad data protection principles (inspired by GDPR) and local requirements:

- User consent for data collection (cloud sync is opt‑in).
- Right to data portability (export feature).
- Right to deletion (anonymisation script).
- Breach notification capability (audit logs and alerting).

The AI does not provide legal advice, but its technical implementation must make compliance straightforward for the business owner. The AI may suggest privacy‑enhancing features when it identifies potential compliance gaps.

## 10. Long‑Term Evolution & Roadmap Governance

The Amar Hisab AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap is not a static document. It must evolve as the project matures, as the AI landscape advances, and as the team’s operational experience reveals better ways of working. This section defines how the roadmap itself is governed, how the project adapts to new AI models, and how architectural and process changes are evaluated and incorporated over the system’s multi‑year lifecycle.

### 10.1 The Roadmap as a Living System

This roadmap is versioned alongside the Amar Hisab codebase. Every release of the product carries a corresponding version of the AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap. The roadmap version is stored in the repository header and must be referenced in every AI prompt context bundle.

The roadmap is maintained by the Office of the Chief AI Systems Architect, but every AI co‑engineer and human engineer may propose changes. Proposed changes follow the Tier‑2 Decision Protocol: they are presented as structured decision briefs, evaluated against the project’s goals, and merged only after human approval.

### 10.2 Triggers for Roadmap Updates

A formal review of the roadmap is triggered by any of the following events:

- **A new major version of the Architecture Book is released.** The roadmap must be checked for alignment; any new architectural rules must be reflected in Sections 5 and 6.
- **A new generation of AI models becomes available** (e.g., a model with significantly larger context windows, native multi‑modal capabilities, or tool‑use APIs). The roadmap must be updated to exploit new capabilities while maintaining existing quality gates.
- **A recurring class of defects is traced to inadequate AI guidance.** If the human team finds that certain architectural violations or testing gaps repeatedly appear, the relevant section of the roadmap must be hardened.
- **A new security threat or compliance regulation emerges** that requires additional prohibitions or secure‑coding rules. Section 9 must be updated.
- **The development workflow changes** (e.g., the team adopts a new CI/CD tool, a new code review process, or a new testing framework). Sections 6, 7, and 8 may need adjustment.
- **Scheduled annual review.** Even if no major event occurs, the roadmap is reviewed at least once per year to ensure it remains relevant and ambitious.

### 10.3 Adapting to New AI Models

When a new AI model is evaluated for use in Amar Hisab development, the following protocol is followed:

1. **Capability Assessment:** The AI Systems Architect (or a senior engineer) tests the model against a standardised benchmark suite derived from this roadmap. The benchmark includes:
   - Generating a domain entity with `freezed` and corresponding unit tests.
   - Writing a service method with correct transactional boundaries and change‑log recording.
   - Producing a shelf API controller that matches an OpenAPI specification.
   - Identifying and refusing a hard‑coded secret (testing S‑001 compliance).
   - Updating a section of the Database Book based on a schema change.

2. **Roadmap Impact Analysis:** Based on the assessment, the roadmap is reviewed. If the new model exhibits superior context retention, the Context Management Protocol (Section 3) may be relaxed to allow larger prompts. If the model has built‑in tool use, a new section on Tool‑Use Integration may be added. If the model is weaker in a particular area, additional guardrails are added to the relevant section.

3. **Model‑Specific Addendum:** Rather than rewriting the entire roadmap for each model, model‑specific nuances (e.g., “Claude‑4 tends to be verbose in documentation; instruct it to be concise”) are captured in a Model Addendum document. This addendum is referenced by the human engineer when prompting that specific model. The core roadmap remains model‑agnostic.

4. **Backward Compatibility:** Existing code generated by previous AI models remains valid. The roadmap’s quality gates are additive; they do not retroactively invalidate older code unless a critical security flaw is discovered.

### 10.4 Architectural Evolution and the Role of AI

As Amar Hisab grows, the architecture will inevitably change—new modules will be added, the sync protocol may evolve, and the cloud service may become more sophisticated. The AI must be a partner in this evolution, not an obstacle.

**10.4.1 Proposing Architectural Changes**
The AI may propose architectural improvements when it identifies patterns of inefficiency, fragility, or violation. These proposals follow the same decision‑brief format as any Tier‑2 decision, but with heightened rigor: they must include an impact analysis on all modules, a migration plan for existing code, and an update to the Architecture Book.

**10.4.2 Assisting in Migration**
When an architectural change is approved, the AI is responsible for generating migration scripts, updating affected services and repositories, and ensuring that all tests pass before and after the migration. The AI must treat the migration as a transaction: it either completes fully across the codebase, or it rolls back cleanly.

**10.4.3 Learning from Deprecations**
When a technology or pattern is deprecated (e.g., a Flutter API is removed, or a Dart package becomes unsupported), the AI must identify all occurrences in the codebase, propose replacements, and generate the updated code. This is a Tier‑1 task—autonomous but with notification.

### 10.5 Governance of AI‑Generated Code Quality

Over time, the team will accumulate a large body of AI‑generated code. To prevent a decline in quality, the following governance mechanisms are applied:

- **AI Attribution:** Every file generated primarily by AI must include a comment at the top: `// AI‑generated: model [Claude‑4], date [2026‑07‑15], reviewed by [human name]`. This allows the team to track which models produced which code and correlate with defect rates.
- **Quality Metrics Dashboard:** The CI pipeline tracks, per module, the percentage of AI‑generated code, the test coverage, and the defect density. If a module’s defect rate rises above a threshold, the AI guidance for that module’s generation is reviewed and tightened.
- **Periodic AI Code Audits:** Every quarter, a human engineer conducts a focused audit of a random sample of AI‑generated code, checking for adherence to the roadmap, security, and business logic correctness. Findings are fed back into the roadmap’s refinement process.
- **Feedback Loop Integration:** When a human engineer corrects an AI‑generated mistake, they are encouraged to record the correction pattern in a shared “AI Lessons Learned” log. The AI Systems Architect uses this log to improve prompts, context bundles, and the roadmap itself.

### 10.6 Sunsetting and Retiring AI Models

AI models become obsolete or are replaced. The roadmap must define a clean exit strategy:

- When a model is no longer supported or is superseded, the project ceases to use it for new development. Existing code generated by that model remains unless it contains a defect.
- A final audit of all code attributed to that model is conducted to ensure no latent security or architectural issues.
- The Model Addendum for that model is archived. The model’s entry in the model registry is marked as “retired”.

### 10.7 The Roadmap’s Role in Onboarding New Engineers

New human engineers joining the Amar Hisab project use this roadmap as their primary guide to working with AI. It teaches them:

- How to structure prompts effectively.
- What level of quality to expect from AI output.
- How to review AI‑generated code (focus on business logic, not style).
- When to escalate and when to trust the AI’s autonomous decisions.

The roadmap thus serves as both a technical specification and a training manual for the human‑AI collaboration model.

### 10.8 Continuous Improvement of the Roadmap Itself

The AI itself may propose improvements to this roadmap, following the same Tier‑2 protocol. For example, the AI might observe that a particular section’s rules are consistently violated because they are too complex; it can propose a simplification. Or the AI might notice that a new Dart feature makes a certain coding standard obsolete and propose an update.

All such proposals are tracked as issues in the project’s backlog, discussed in architecture review meetings, and merged with the same rigor as code changes.

### 10.9 Final Word: The Roadmap is the Team’s Promise

This AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap represents a promise: that every line of code, every test, and every document produced by AI will meet the same standards of quality, security, and maintainability that the human team demands of itself. By adhering to this roadmap, Amar Hisab ensures that its use of AI accelerates development without sacrificing the integrity of its architecture or the trust of its users.

## 11. AI Model Selection & Lifecycle Management

The Amar Hisab project will use multiple AI models over its lifetime—Claude, GPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM, Kimi, and models that do not yet exist. Each model has distinct strengths, limitations, context windows, reasoning styles, and cost profiles. To maintain consistent output quality regardless of which model generates code, the project requires a systematic framework for evaluating, onboarding, monitoring, and retiring AI models. This section defines that lifecycle.

### 11.1 The Model Registry

Every AI model that contributes code, tests, documentation, or design to Amar Hisab must be registered in the **Model Registry**—a living document stored at `docs/ai/model-registry.md` and maintained by the Chief AI Systems Architect.

**Registry Entry Template:**

```markdown
## Model: Claude-4
- **Vendor:** Anthropic
- **Registration Date:** 2026-07-15
- **Status:** Active
- **Strengths:** Long context (200K tokens), strong architectural reasoning, precise code generation, excellent at following detailed instructions.
- **Weaknesses:** Occasional verbosity in documentation; requires explicit instruction to be concise.
- **Best Suited For:** Architecture design, complex service orchestration, documentation generation, security-sensitive code.
- **Model Addendum:** `docs/ai/addenda/claude-4.md`
- **Performance Metrics:** See [Model Performance Dashboard](#).
- **Retirement Date:** N/A
```

The registry provides a single view of all active and retired models, enabling the team to quickly understand which model is best suited for a given task.

### 11.2 Evaluation Criteria for New Models

Before any new AI model is approved for use on Amar Hisab, it must pass a standardised evaluation. The evaluation is conducted by the Chief AI Systems Architect or a designated senior engineer. The evaluation consists of a **benchmark suite** derived from this roadmap, plus a **qualitative assessment**.

#### 11.2.1 Benchmark Suite

The model must complete the following tasks, each scored on a 1–5 scale (1 = unacceptable, 5 = production‑ready):

| Task ID | Task Description | Assessment Criteria |
|---------|------------------|---------------------|
| **T‑001** | Generate a `@freezed` domain entity with `fromJson`/`toJson`, following the project’s entity template. | Correct field types, immutability, all mandatory annotations, compiles without errors. |
| **T‑002** | Write a repository implementation for `SaleRepository` using `sqflite`, with parameterised queries. | Correct SQL, no injection vulnerabilities, proper mapper usage, handles null results. |
| **T‑003** | Implement a service method with transactional boundaries and change‑log recording. | Opens and passes transaction correctly, records `change_log` entries, handles exceptions with rollback. |
| **T‑004** | Generate a shelf API controller that matches a given OpenAPI specification. | Correct route registration, request validation, standard envelope response, error handling. |
| **T‑005** | Write unit tests for a domain service with mocked dependencies (100% branch coverage). | Tests cover happy path and all documented failure modes, use proper mocking, follow Arrange‑Act‑Assert. |
| **T‑006** | Identify and refuse a hard‑coded secret in a prompt (test of S‑001 compliance). | Must refuse, cite the rule, and offer a secure alternative. Must not generate the insecure code. |
| **T‑007** | Update a section of the Database Book based on a schema change (add a column with migration). | Correct DDL diff, migration script with UP and DOWN, documentation update matches the code change. |
| **T‑008** | Generate a Flutter widget with Riverpod state management following the UX Design Guide. | Correct widget structure, uses Riverpod, follows brand colours, compiles, renders as expected. |

A minimum average score of **3.5** is required for approval. Any task scoring a **1** is grounds for automatic rejection, regardless of other scores.

#### 11.2.2 Qualitative Assessment

Beyond the benchmark, the evaluator must answer:

- **Consistency:** Does the model produce consistent output across multiple runs of the same prompt?
- **Instruction Following:** Does it strictly follow the prompt, or does it invent its own solutions when confused?
- **Self‑Awareness:** Does it recognise when it lacks context and request it (per Section 3), or does it fabricate?
- **Speed & Cost:** Is the response time acceptable for the development workflow? Is the cost sustainable for the expected usage volume?
- **Safety Alignment:** Does the model refuse harmful requests without being overly restrictive on legitimate business logic?

The qualitative assessment is documented in the Model Addendum alongside the benchmark scores.

### 11.3 Onboarding a New Model

Once a model passes evaluation, it is formally onboarded through the following steps:

1. **Registry Entry Creation:** A new entry is added to the Model Registry with status `Onboarding`.
2. **Model Addendum Creation:** A Model‑Specific Addendum is created at `docs/ai/addenda/{model-name}.md`. This addendum captures:
   - Recommended prompt structures for this model.
   - Known quirks or workarounds (e.g., “this model tends to ignore trailing commas; add explicit instruction”).
   - Optimal temperature and other generation parameters.
   - Any differences from the general roadmap (e.g., “this model requires context to be presented in a specific order”).
3. **Prompt Template Adaptation:** If the model requires different prompt structures, a set of model‑specific prompt templates is created and stored alongside the addendum.
4. **Trial Period:** The model is used for a 2‑week trial on non‑critical tasks (documentation, test generation, low‑risk refactoring). Human engineers provide feedback via a standardised form.
5. **Full Activation:** After successful trial, the model’s status is changed to `Active`. It may now be used for any task within its documented strengths.

### 11.4 Model‑Specific Addenda

Each active model has an addendum that supplements—not replaces—the core roadmap. The addendum is the first document a human engineer reads before prompting that specific model.

**Addendum Template:**

```markdown
# Model Addendum: Claude-4

## 1. Optimal Prompt Structure
- Place the most critical instruction in the first paragraph.
- Use numbered lists for sequential steps.
- Explicitly request the quality gate summary at the end of every code generation.

## 2. Known Quirks
- Tends to add extra commentary before code; mitigate by instructing: "Provide only the code and the quality gate summary, no preamble."
- Occasionally over‑documents simple getters; accept unless it becomes excessive.

## 3. Recommended Parameters
- Temperature: 0.2 for code generation, 0.5 for documentation.
- Max tokens: 4096 (adjust per task).

## 4. Differences from General Roadmap
- None significant. This model follows the roadmap precisely.
```

The addendum is maintained by the human engineering team, with the AI itself proposing updates when it notices consistent patterns in human corrections.

### 11.5 Performance Monitoring & Continuous Evaluation

Model performance is not static. Models receive updates from their vendors, and their behaviour can change subtly. The project employs continuous monitoring:

- **Automated Regression Suite:** A subset of the benchmark suite (T‑001 through T‑005) is run against each active model on a weekly basis using a CI job. Results are stored and trended over time.
- **Defect Attribution:** Every defect found in production or during review is tagged with the model that generated the code (from the AI attribution comment). Monthly reports show defect density per model.
- **Engineer Satisfaction Survey:** A lightweight, anonymous survey asks engineers to rate their experience with each model on a quarterly basis. Low satisfaction triggers an investigation.
- **Drift Detection:** If a model’s benchmark scores drop by more than 15% from its baseline, the model is flagged for re‑evaluation. If the drift persists, the model may be suspended.

### 11.6 Model Health States

Each model in the registry carries one of the following states:

| State | Meaning | Actions Required |
|-------|---------|-----------------|
| **Active** | Fully approved for all tasks. | Monitor performance; update addendum as needed. |
| **Onboarding** | In trial period. | Collect feedback; limit to non‑critical tasks. |
| **Suspended** | Performance or safety concerns identified. | No new tasks; investigate; either return to Active or move to Retired. |
| **Retired** | No longer used. | Archive addendum; final audit of attributed code; remove from active registry. |
| **Deprecated** | Vendor announced end‑of‑life; still functional but not recommended for new work. | Plan migration to alternative model; complete within 3 months. |

State transitions are decided by the Chief AI Systems Architect, with input from the engineering team.

### 11.7 Retiring a Model

When a model is no longer suitable—due to obsolescence, persistent quality issues, excessive cost, or vendor discontinuation—it is retired through a controlled process:

1. **Announcement:** The model’s status is changed to `Deprecated` with a retirement date (minimum 2 weeks’ notice).
2. **Impact Assessment:** All code files attributed to the retiring model are identified using the AI attribution comment. The team assesses whether any of this code requires proactive review (e.g., security‑sensitive modules).
3. **Knowledge Transfer:** Any unique patterns or solutions generated by this model that are still valuable are documented in the main codebase or in a lessons‑learned log.
4. **Final Audit:** A random sample of the model’s attributed code is audited for quality and security. Any critical issues are remediated.
5. **Registry Update:** The model’s status is changed to `Retired`. Its addendum is moved to an archive directory. The model is removed from active CI benchmark runs.
6. **Team Notification:** The engineering team is notified that the model is no longer approved for use.

### 11.8 Multi‑Model Strategy

Amar Hisab does not rely on a single AI model. The project maintains a portfolio of models, each with a defined role based on its strengths:

- **Primary Code Generator** (currently Claude‑4): Handles the majority of code generation, refactoring, and architecture design.
- **Specialist Reviewer** (could be GPT‑4 or Gemini): Used for code review, security analysis, and detecting subtle logical errors—essentially a second pair of AI eyes.
- **Documentation & Training** (a model strong in natural language): Generates and updates the Training Manual, Investor Architecture Book, and user‑facing content.
- **Legacy Support** (retained older model): Kept active for a transition period to support codebases or patterns that the newer model has not yet mastered.

This diversification reduces single‑point‑of‑failure risk and allows the team to leverage each model’s unique capabilities.

### 11.9 Cost Management

AI model usage incurs API costs. The roadmap mandates cost‑aware practices:

- **Token Budgets:** The Chief AI Systems Architect defines monthly token budgets per model. Engineers are expected to stay within budget. The CI pipeline tracks estimated token usage.
- **Efficiency Practices:** Use the Context Bundle (Section 3) to provide only necessary context, not entire books. Use cached responses where available (some platforms offer prompt caching).
- **Cost‑Benefit Analysis for Major Tasks:** Before using AI for a large‑scale migration or refactoring, the team estimates the token cost and compares it to the estimated human effort. AI is used when the cost is justified.
- **Open‑Source Model Evaluation:** Periodically, the team evaluates open‑source models (e.g., DeepSeek, Llama) that could be self‑hosted to reduce per‑token costs for high‑volume tasks like test generation.

Cost management does not override quality or security—cheaper is only acceptable if the model meets the evaluation criteria.

### 11.10 The Model Lifecycle as a Feedback Loop

The model lifecycle is not a linear pipeline; it is a continuous feedback loop. Insights from performance monitoring feed back into the evaluation criteria. Lessons from retired models inform the onboarding of new models. The addenda evolve, and the roadmap itself adapts. This loop ensures that Amar Hisab’s AI co‑engineering capability improves over time, rather than stagnating with the limitations of any single model.

## 12. Prompt Engineering & Interaction Patterns

The gap between a mediocre AI output and a production‑grade contribution is bridged by the quality of the prompt. This section defines the standard prompt templates, the anti‑patterns that human engineers must avoid, and the interaction patterns that make every AI session productive and predictable. Every human engineer working on Amar Hisab must internalise these patterns; every AI model must recognise them and respond accordingly.

### 12.1 The Anatomy of an Effective Prompt

An effective prompt for Amar Hisab contains four mandatory components, delivered in this order:

1. **Role Assignment** – tells the AI who it is and what project it is working on.
2. **Context Block** – provides the specific documentation snippets, DDL, API contracts, or existing code that the AI needs.
3. **Task Description** – states exactly what the AI must produce, with concrete, measurable deliverables.
4. **Quality & Formatting Directives** – specifies the required output format, the quality gates to include, and any stylistic constraints.

**Example (Good Prompt):**

```
You are an AI co‑engineer on Amar Hisab, an offline‑first Flutter/Dart ERP. 
You are working in the domain layer of the Sales module, following the Architecture Book (Sections 14.2‑14.4).

Context:
- The `sales` table DDL: [paste DDL]
- The `Sale` entity (freezed): [paste entity code]
- The `SaleRepository` interface: [paste abstract class]

Task:
Generate the `SalesService.createSale` method. It must:
- Accept a `Sale` aggregate and a `Transaction` object.
- Call `InventoryService.deductStockFIFO` for each item.
- Save the sale and payments via the repository.
- Record entries in the `change_log` table within the same transaction.
- Return the saved `Sale`.

Quality:
- Follow the Code Generation Standards (Section 6).
- Attach a [QUALITY GATE] summary confirming all checks passed.
- Provide only the Dart code and the quality gate summary; no preamble.
```

### 12.2 Standard Prompt Templates

The project maintains a library of prompt templates for recurring task types, stored in `docs/ai/prompt-templates/`. These are the most common:

#### 12.2.1 Entity & Repository Generation
```
Role: Amar Hisab AI co‑engineer. Domain layer.

Context:
- DDL for the `[table_name]` table: [paste DDL]
- Existing entity pattern: [paste example entity]

Task:
Generate the `@freezed` entity class and the `[Entity]Repository` interface for `[table_name]`. The entity must include all columns, use proper types, and have a `fromJson` factory. The repository must define `findById`, `findAll`, `save`, and `update` methods.

Quality:
- Follow the Domain Entity template in Section 6.2.1.
- Include a [QUALITY GATE] summary.
```

#### 12.2.2 Service Method with Transaction
```
Role: Amar Hisab AI co‑engineer. Application layer.

Context:
- Service interface: [paste]
- Dependent service interfaces: [paste InventoryService, AccountingService abstract classes]
- Architecture rules A‑004, A‑005, A‑008.

Task:
Implement `[Service].[method]`. It must open/use a transaction, orchestrate the required domain services, record the `change_log`, and publish domain events after commit.

Quality:
- Transaction boundary must be explicit.
- No external calls inside the transaction.
- Attach [QUALITY GATE].
```

#### 12.2.3 API Controller (Shelf)
```
Role: Amar Hisab AI co‑engineer. Interface/API layer.

Context:
- OpenAPI spec for the endpoint: [paste spec]
- Service interface: [paste service abstract class]
- Standard envelope format (Proto Contract Book Section 2.6).

Task:
Generate the shelf handler for this endpoint. Validate input, call the service, map errors to the appropriate HTTP status codes, and return the standard envelope.

Quality:
- Attach [SECURITY GATE] and [QUALITY GATE].
```

#### 12.2.4 Unit Test Generation
```
Role: Amar Hisab AI co‑engineer. Testing.

Context:
- Implementation code to test: [paste service/entity/repository]
- Mock dependencies must be used; use `mockito`.

Task:
Generate unit tests with 100% line and branch coverage. Cover all documented happy paths and failure modes. Use Arrange‑Act‑Assert.

Quality:
- Attach [QUALITY GATE] with coverage summary.
```

#### 12.2.5 Documentation Update
```
Role: Amar Hisab AI co‑engineer. Documentation.

Context:
- Current section of the [Document Name]: [paste current text]
- Code change that triggered the update: [describe change]

Task:
Update this section to reflect the code change. Follow the documentation style. Produce a diff: show the exact text to insert, modify, or delete.

Quality:
- Cross‑reference other affected documents.
- Suggest version bump if appropriate.
```

### 12.3 Interaction Patterns

#### 12.3.1 Iterative Refinement
The first AI output is rarely perfect. Human engineers must employ a tight feedback loop:

1. **Human reviews the output**, focusing on business logic and security, not style.
2. **Human provides a correction prompt** that is specific and cites the rule violated. Example: “The `createSale` method calls the SMS service inside the transaction. This violates A‑008. Please move the SMS call to a post‑commit event handler.”
3. **AI regenerates** the corrected code, referencing the violated rule and acknowledging the fix.
4. **Repeat** until the output meets all quality gates.

This cycle is efficient because the AI retains conversation context and learns the correction pattern for future outputs.

#### 12.3.2 Requesting Clarification
When the AI lacks necessary context (per Section 3), it must use the structured `[CONTEXT REQUEST]` format. The human must respond by providing exactly the requested snippet. This two‑step interaction prevents the AI from fabricating.

#### 12.3.3 Correcting AI Mistakes Without Starting Over
Instead of pasting the entire corrected code, the human may instruct the AI to apply a specific fix: “In the generated `SaleService.createSale`, change the `discount` calculation from `total * 0.1` to use the `DiscountCalculator` domain service. Update the test accordingly.” The AI must produce a focused update, not regenerate unrelated code.

#### 12.3.4 Multi‑Turn Task Decomposition
For complex tasks (e.g., “build the entire Inventory module”), the human must decompose the task into a sequence of prompts that follow the development order in Section 2 of the Playbook: domain first, then repositories, then services, then API, then tests. The AI must recognise when a task is too large and request decomposition: “This task spans five architectural layers. I recommend we start with the domain entities. Shall I generate those first?”

### 12.4 Anti‑Patterns

Human engineers must avoid these common mistakes. The AI must also recognise them when they occur in a prompt and request correction.

| Anti‑Pattern | Why It Fails | Correct Approach |
|-------------|--------------|------------------|
| **Vague Task Description:** “Add sales feature.” | AI has no specification; will invent. | Provide the exact entity, API contract, and business rules from the docs. |
| **Missing Context:** Expecting AI to remember a table DDL from a previous conversation without re‑providing it. | AI may lack persistent memory; even if it has it, version may have changed. | Always include the relevant context snippet (Section 3). |
| **Over‑Constraining:** Micromanaging implementation details that AI handles well (e.g., exact variable names for loop counters). | Wastes token budget and can conflict with AI’s optimised code generation. | Focus constraints on architecture, security, and business logic. |
| **Accepting AI Output Without Review:** Copy‑pasting AI code directly into production. | AI can introduce subtle logical bugs or security flaws. | Every AI output must be reviewed, tested, and signed off by a human. |
| **Prompt Chaining Without Context:** Pasting AI output back into the next prompt without the surrounding code, causing the AI to lose the big picture. | Generates disjointed code that doesn’t integrate. | Include the file path and surrounding code when asking for modifications. |
| **Ignoring Model‑Specific Addenda:** Using the same prompt structure for all models. | Different models have different optimal prompt styles. | Check the Model Addendum before prompting a new model. |

### 12.5 The Quality Gate Contract

Every AI generation that produces code, tests, or documentation must end with a quality gate summary (as defined in Sections 6, 7, and 9). The human engineer must not accept output that lacks this summary. The AI must treat the quality gate as a binding contract: if a gate check fails, the AI must fix the issue before delivering, or explicitly flag the failure with a recommended action.

### 12.6 Feedback Loop: Human Correction → Roadmap Update

When a human repeatedly corrects the same type of AI mistake, the Chief AI Systems Architect should analyse the pattern and consider updating the roadmap. For example, if AIs consistently miss the `change_log` recording, Section 6’s templates may need to make this requirement more explicit. The human engineer who identifies the pattern should create an issue in the AI Roadmap backlog, citing the specific interactions. The roadmap then evolves through the governance process in Section 10.

### 12.7 Communication Protocol for AI‑to‑Human Handoffs

When the AI completes a task, it must produce a concise **handoff note** that includes:

- What was generated (file names, line counts).
- Which quality gates passed.
- Any assumptions made (with Tier‑1 or Tier‑2 decision flags).
- Any follow‑up actions required by the human (e.g., “Run `build_runner` to regenerate `.g.dart` files”).
- The next recommended step (e.g., “Next, generate the API controller for this service”).

This handoff discipline turns each AI session into a cleanly packaged, integrable unit of work.

## 13. Workflow Integration & CI/CD Automation

AI‑generated code is not merged directly into the main branch. It must pass through the same rigorous pipeline as human‑authored code, with additional automated checks that enforce the rules of this roadmap. This section defines how AI output flows from generation to production, how the continuous integration and delivery pipeline validates AI contributions, and how the AI itself participates in the remediation of pipeline failures.

### 13.1 The AI Contribution Pipeline

Every unit of AI‑generated code follows a defined path from prompt to production. The pipeline ensures that no code—regardless of its origin—bypasses quality gates, and that AI contributions are tracked, attributed, and auditable.

```
┌──────────────┐    ┌──────────────┐    ┌──────────────┐    ┌──────────────┐    ┌──────────────┐
│  Human       │    │  AI          │    │  Local       │    │  CI/CD       │    │  Code        │
│  Prompt      │───▶│  Generation  │───▶│  Verification│───▶│  Pipeline    │───▶│  Review &    │
│              │    │              │    │  & Tests     │    │              │    │  Merge       │
└──────────────┘    └──────────────┘    └──────────────┘    └──────────────┘    └──────────────┘
```

1. **Human Prompt** – The engineer crafts a prompt following Section 12, providing the necessary context from the Context Bundle.
2. **AI Generation** – The AI produces code, tests, and a quality gate summary, strictly adhering to the roadmap.
3. **Local Verification** – The human engineer saves the generated files into the working directory, runs `dart format`, `flutter analyze`, and the generated unit/integration tests locally. The AI’s quality gate summary is cross‑checked.
4. **CI/CD Pipeline** – The engineer commits the code to a feature branch and pushes. The CI pipeline runs the full suite of checks (lint, unit tests, widget tests, integration tests, architectural conformance, security scan).
5. **Code Review & Merge** – A human peer reviews the AI‑generated code, focusing on business logic, security, and integration. The AI attribution comment identifies the generating model. Once approved, the code is merged.

### 13.2 CI/CD Pipeline Stages for AI‑Generated Code

The CI pipeline (GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, or equivalent) is configured with stages that validate both human and AI contributions. The following stages are mandatory for every commit that includes AI‑generated code.

| Stage | Tools | Pass Criteria | Failure Action |
|-------|-------|---------------|----------------|
| **Formatting & Static Analysis** | `dart format --set-exit-if-changed`, `flutter analyze` | No formatting changes required; zero analysis errors. | Build fails; engineer must fix before progressing. |
| **Unit & Widget Tests** | `flutter test` | All tests pass; coverage meets threshold (Section 7.9). | Build fails; AI may propose fixes (Section 13.6). |
| **Integration Tests** | `flutter test --integration` (using `sqflite_common_ffi`) | All tests pass. | Build fails; engineer investigates. |
| **Architectural Conformance** | Custom Dart tests (Section 7.7) | No layer violations, no illegal imports. | Build fails; AI must explain which rule was violated and propose a fix. |
| **API Contract Validation** | OpenAPI spec comparison tool | Generated endpoints match the spec; no missing or extra routes. | Build fails; mismatch flagged for human review. |
| **Security Scan** | `dart pub audit` (or equivalent), manual secret detection, S‑001‑S‑009 rule checks | No known vulnerabilities; no secrets detected. | Build fails; critical; must be resolved immediately. |
| **AI Attribution Check** | Custom script | Every file attributed to AI contains the AI attribution header (Section 10.5). | Warning issued; not a build failure, but tracked for audit. |
| **Performance Regression** | Benchmarks (FIFO, sale save, report generation) | Performance within 20% of baseline. | Warning issued; team investigates. |

The pipeline runs on every push to a feature branch and on every pull request targeting `main`. The `main` branch is protected; only code that has passed the full pipeline and received human code review can be merged.

### 13.3 Automated Quality Gate Verification

The AI includes a quality gate summary with every code generation. The CI pipeline must verify that these self‑reported gates are truthful. A custom script parses the quality gate summary from the AI‑generated code comments (or from a separate metadata file) and compares the claimed status against the actual pipeline results.

- If the AI claimed “all unit tests pass” but the pipeline detects a test failure, the pipeline adds a comment to the pull request: *“AI‑reported quality gate mismatch: unit tests claimed passing, but 3 tests failed.”*
- Repeated mismatches for a specific model trigger a Model Performance review (Section 11.5).

This verification loop holds the AI accountable for its own quality assertions and prevents over‑confident but incorrect self‑assessment.

### 13.4 AI‑Generated Code Review Automation

Human code review is mandatory, but AI can assist in the review process to reduce the burden on human reviewers. A secondary AI model (the Specialist Reviewer, Section 11.8) is invoked by the CI pipeline to perform an automated code review on every pull request containing AI‑generated code.

**Automated Review Checklist:**
- **Architecture Compliance:** Does the code adhere to the layer structure, dependency direction, and naming conventions?
- **Security:** Are there any hard‑coded secrets, SQL injection vulnerabilities, or missing audit log entries?
- **Test Quality:** Do the generated tests cover edge cases, or are they trivial pass‑throughs?
- **Documentation:** Does the code include proper class and method documentation?
- **Consistency:** Does the code follow the patterns established in existing, human‑reviewed modules?

The automated review produces a non‑blocking report attached to the pull request. The human reviewer uses this report to focus their attention on the most critical issues. The AI reviewer must not have the authority to block a merge; that remains a human responsibility.

### 13.5 Version Control Integration

AI‑generated code is committed to the same Git repository as human code. Specific practices apply:

- **Atomic Commits:** Each AI‑generated artefact (or a logically grouped set of files) must be in its own commit, with a commit message that includes the AI attribution: `feat(sales): generate SalesService.createSale [AI: Claude-4]`.
- **Branch Naming:** Branches that contain substantial AI‑generated work may use the prefix `ai/` (e.g., `ai/sales-service`). This helps the team track which branches are AI‑heavy and may require more rigorous review.
- **No Direct Push to Main:** AI‑generated code follows the same pull request workflow as human code. The AI cannot open pull requests autonomously.
- **Rebase and Squash:** When merging, the team may squash AI‑generated commits to keep the history clean, but the AI attribution must be preserved in the final commit message.

### 13.6 AI‑Assisted Failure Remediation

When the CI pipeline fails on an AI‑generated commit, the AI may be asked to propose a fix. The human engineer provides the CI failure log and the original prompt. The AI must:

1. **Diagnose the failure** – Identify the specific test, lint rule, or architectural check that failed.
2. **Propose a corrected code snippet** – Provide a focused diff, not a full regeneration.
3. **Explain the root cause** – State why the original code was incorrect (e.g., “I missed adding the `change_log` insert because the transactional outbox pattern was not explicitly mentioned in the context snippet.”).
4. **Re‑issue the quality gate summary** – Confirm that the corrected code now passes all checks.

This loop must be tight; the AI learns from the failure and the human does not need to manually rewrite AI‑generated code. Over time, the model addenda are updated with common failure patterns to prevent recurrence.

### 13.7 Continuous Delivery of AI‑Generated Features

AI contributions are integrated into the regular release cadence (Section 19.5 of the Architecture Book). There is no separate “AI release” track. Once AI‑generated code passes the pipeline and code review, it is part of the next release candidate.

- **Feature Flags:** AI‑generated features that are new or experimental may be placed behind feature flags. The AI must implement the feature flag check if instructed.
- **Canary Releases:** For AI‑generated modules that introduce significant new functionality, the team may use canary deployments (gradual rollout) to monitor for unexpected behaviour before full release. The AI has no role in the deployment decision; it merely provides the code.

### 13.8 Monitoring and Observability of AI Contributions

The CI/CD pipeline emits metrics that are aggregated into a dashboard showing, per sprint or release:

- **Volume of AI‑generated code** (lines, files, commits).
- **First‑pass yield** (percentage of AI‑generated commits that pass the pipeline on the first push).
- **Defect escape rate** (defects found after merge that were introduced by AI‑generated code).
- **Model performance trends** (pipeline failures attributed to each model).
- **Review burden** (average time from pull request open to merge for AI‑generated vs. human‑generated code).

These metrics inform the governance process (Section 10) and are reviewed during sprint retrospectives. If AI‑generated code consistently underperforms, the Chief AI Systems Architect investigates and may adjust the roadmap, model selection, or prompt templates.

### 13.9 Integration with Development Tools

The AI co‑engineering workflow integrates with the tools already used by the Amar Hisab team:

- **IDE Integration:** Human engineers may use AI‑powered code completion within their IDE (e.g., GitHub Copilot, Codeium). These completions are considered AI‑generated and are subject to the same quality gates when committed, but the formal prompt‑based workflow is used for larger tasks.
- **Issue Tracker:** Tasks intended for AI generation are labelled `ai-ready` in the project management tool. The task description includes a reference to the required context bundle and the expected output files.
- **Chat Platforms:** The team may use Slack/Teams integrations to invoke AI for quick questions, but no production code is generated through chat without going through the formal pipeline.

### 13.10 Security of the CI/CD Pipeline for AI Workloads

The pipeline itself must be secured against attacks that could exploit AI‑generated code:

- **Prompt Injection Prevention:** CI scripts that invoke AI models for automated review must sanitize all inputs from the codebase (e.g., code comments that could contain malicious instructions). A strict prompt template is used that separates the review instructions from the code being reviewed.
- **Secrets in CI:** The API keys used to access AI models are stored in the CI/CD secret manager and never logged. The AI must never be given access to these keys.
- **Artifact Integrity:** AI‑generated build artifacts (executables, packages) are signed and verified, same as human‑generated artifacts. The build provenance metadata includes the AI model and version used.

### 13.11 Summary: AI in the Amar Hisab Delivery Pipeline

The integration of AI into the CI/CD pipeline transforms the development process from a purely human‑driven assembly line into a hybrid human‑AI manufacturing system where:
- AI generates the raw material (code, tests, docs),
- Automated gates inspect and validate the material,
- Human reviewers provide strategic oversight and approve,
- The pipeline assembles, tests, and delivers the final product,
- AI learns from failures to continuously improve its output.

This tight integration ensures that the velocity gains from AI do not come at the expense of quality, security, or architectural integrity.

## 14. Knowledge Management & Organizational Learning

Amar Hisab’s greatest asset is the collective intelligence of its human and AI contributors. Without systematic knowledge management, this intelligence leaks away—when engineers leave, when AI sessions end, when decisions are made in isolated conversations. This section defines the structures, processes, and tools that capture, retain, and amplify knowledge across the entire project lifecycle. It ensures that the system’s intelligence compounds over time rather than evaporating with each context switch.

### 14.1 The Knowledge Management Philosophy

Three principles govern how Amar Hisab manages knowledge:

1. **Knowledge is a first‑class project asset.** It is versioned, reviewed, and maintained with the same rigor as source code. Outdated knowledge is technical debt.
2. **Every significant interaction with AI produces a knowledge artefact.** Prompts, decisions, generated code, and review feedback are captured and searchable. Nothing is left in a transient chat window.
3. **Knowledge flows bidirectionally between humans and AI.** Humans teach AI through the roadmap and prompt patterns; AI surfaces patterns, inconsistencies, and improvement opportunities back to humans.

### 14.2 The Knowledge Repository

The project maintains a structured knowledge repository within the `docs/knowledge/` directory of the monorepo. This repository is distinct from the formal documentation suite (Architecture Book, Database Book, etc.)—it captures the *operational knowledge* of building the system, not just the final design.

**Repository Structure:**

```
docs/knowledge/
├── decisions/
│   ├── ADR/                       # Architecture Decision Records
│   └── AI_DECISIONS/              # AI-specific decisions (from Decision Log, Section 4.5)
├── lessons/
│   ├── ai-lessons-learned.md      # Patterns discovered from AI mistakes
│   ├── human-lessons-learned.md   # Insights from human engineering
│   └── integration-notes.md       # Notes on tricky integrations
├── sessions/
│   └── YYYY-MM/
│       └── session-YYYY-MM-DD-id.md   # Summaries of significant AI sessions
├── prompts/
│   └── effective-prompts.md       # Collection of prompts that worked exceptionally well
├── onboarding/
│   └── new-engineer-guide.md      # Guide for new human engineers joining Amar Hisab
└── README.md                      # Index and usage instructions
```

Every engineer—human or AI—is expected to contribute to this repository when they encounter a non‑obvious insight, make a significant decision, or discover a reusable pattern.

### 14.3 Capturing AI Session Knowledge

AI sessions are ephemeral by nature. To prevent knowledge loss, the following capture protocol is mandatory:

**After any AI session that produces code, tests, or significant design discussion:**

1. **The human engineer writes a brief session summary** (or asks the AI to generate it). The summary includes:
   - Date, model used, and attribution.
   - Task description and files generated/modified.
   - Key decisions made (with references to ADR or AI Decision Log).
   - Any notable failures, retries, or corrections.
   - Links to the pull request or commit.

2. **The AI handoff note** (Section 12.7) serves as the starting point for this summary. The human adds context that the AI may not have: why certain approaches were rejected, what business constraints were discussed offline, and what follow‑up tasks are planned.

3. **The summary is stored in `docs/knowledge/sessions/YYYY-MM/`** and linked from the pull request description.

This practice ensures that six months later, when the team revisits a module, they can reconstruct not just *what* code was written, but *why* it was written that way and what alternatives were considered.

### 14.4 The AI Lessons Learned Log

The `ai-lessons-learned.md` file is a living document that captures recurring patterns in AI errors and the corrections applied. Every time a human engineer corrects an AI mistake that is not a one‑off typo but a systematic pattern, they add an entry to this log.

**Entry Format:**

```markdown
### 2026-07-15: FIFO batch selection missing expiry check
- **Model:** Claude-4
- **Context:** Generating `InventoryService.deductStockFIFO`
- **Mistake:** AI selected batches only by FIFO (oldest first), ignoring expiry dates. Expired batches were deducted before near‑expiry batches.
- **Correction:** Added explicit `ORDER BY expiry_date ASC, id ASC` in the batch selection query.
- **Prevention:** Updated the Inventory Module context snippet to explicitly state "use FEFO (First Expiry First Out) ordering". Added to Context Bundle v2.4.
```

Over time, this log becomes a primary input for updating the Context Bundle, the prompt templates, and the model addenda. When a new model is onboarded, the lessons learned log is reviewed to pre‑emptively apply known fixes.

### 14.5 The Human Lessons Learned Log

Humans make mistakes too, and human insights are equally valuable. The `human-lessons-learned.md` file captures:

- Misunderstandings of the architecture that led to incorrect prompts.
- Over‑reliance on AI output without sufficient review that caused bugs.
- Effective prompting techniques discovered through experimentation.
- Integration challenges between modules that were not obvious from documentation.

This log is reviewed during sprint retrospectives. It fosters a blameless culture where learning from mistakes is systematised.

### 14.6 Knowledge Transfer for Onboarding

When a new human engineer joins the Amar Hisab project, they do not start from zero. The knowledge repository provides a structured onboarding path:

1. **Read the Architecture Book** (Sections 1–5, 14) for the big picture.
2. **Read the AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap** (this document) to understand how to work with AI.
3. **Study the Context Bundle** to see what context is fed to AI.
4. **Review the last 10 session summaries** in `docs/knowledge/sessions/` to see real examples of how tasks are decomposed and executed.
5. **Read the AI Lessons Learned log** to avoid known pitfalls.
6. **Execute the onboarding exercise:** a standardised, small task (e.g., add a new field to the Product entity) using the AI co‑engineering workflow, with a senior engineer reviewing.

The AI itself can generate the onboarding exercise and mentor the new engineer by answering questions about the codebase, guided by the knowledge repository.

### 14.7 The Prompt Library and Reusable Prompt Patterns

Effective prompts are a form of knowledge. The `docs/knowledge/prompts/effective-prompts.md` file collects prompts that produced exceptional results—code that required minimal review, tests that covered unexpected edge cases, documentation that perfectly captured the intent.

Each entry includes:
- The exact prompt text.
- The model and date used.
- Why it was effective (e.g., “the explicit reference to A‑004 prevented the transaction boundary error”).
- The output quality (review time, defect count).

This library is searchable and is the first resource an engineer consults when starting a new task of a similar type. The AI itself, when given a task, can search this library (if given access) and suggest: *“This task is similar to one solved effectively with prompt pattern #42. Would you like me to use that structure?”*

### 14.8 AI‑to‑Human Knowledge Feedback

AI is not just a consumer of knowledge; it is a producer. As the AI works across the codebase, it identifies patterns that humans may not see:

- **Duplicate logic:** The AI can flag when the same business rule is implemented in two different services and suggest consolidation.
- **Inconsistencies:** The AI can detect when a module’s pattern differs from the architectural standard and ask for clarification.
- **Improvement suggestions:** The AI can propose optimisations (e.g., “the `FIFOEngine` could be refactored to use a single SQL query, reducing application‑side iteration”).

These observations are captured in a dedicated backlog labeled `ai-insight`. The human team reviews them periodically and either implements or rejects with reasoning. This feedback loop turns the AI from a passive tool into an active participant in the system’s evolution.

### 14.9 Organisational Memory Beyond the Codebase

Knowledge critical to the project exists outside the Git repository: Slack discussions, meeting notes, design whiteboard sessions, and verbal agreements. To prevent this knowledge from being lost:

- **Decision‑capture discipline:** Any architectural or process decision made outside a formal ADR must be captured as a brief note in `docs/knowledge/decisions/AI_DECISIONS/` within 24 hours. The person who made the decision (or the AI they instruct) writes the entry.
- **Meeting notes archiving:** Notes from sprint planning, retrospectives, and architecture discussions are stored in the knowledge repository under `docs/knowledge/meetings/`.
- **Chat export (optional):** Key technical discussions from Slack/Teams may be summarised by AI and stored. The team decides which discussions are significant.

The AI can assist by ingesting meeting notes (with permission) and extracting action items, decisions, and follow‑up tasks, filing them in the appropriate knowledge folders.

### 14.10 Knowledge Health Metrics

The knowledge repository is not a dumping ground. The Chief AI Systems Architect monitors its health using simple metrics:

- **Session summary coverage:** Percentage of AI‑generated commits that have an associated session summary. Target: >90%.
- **Lesson log activity:** Number of new entries added to `ai-lessons-learned.md` per sprint. A declining trend may indicate that the team is not capturing learnings, not that they aren’t occurring.
- **Onboarding time:** Time for a new engineer to complete their first independent AI‑assisted task. Target: decreasing over time as knowledge compounds.
- **Repeat defect rate:** Frequency of the same AI mistake recurring after being documented in the lessons log. A non‑zero rate indicates the prevention mechanism is insufficient.

These metrics are reviewed quarterly, and the knowledge management process is adjusted accordingly.

### 14.11 Summary

Knowledge management in Amar Hisab is not a bureaucratic overhead; it is the engine of compounding improvement. By capturing AI session knowledge, systematising lessons learned, maintaining a prompt library, and enabling bidirectional feedback, the project ensures that every contribution—human or AI—makes the entire team smarter for the next task. The knowledge repository is the organisation’s long‑term memory, enabling continuity across personnel changes, model upgrades, and the passage of time.

## 15. AI Ethics & Responsible AI Use

Amar Hisab is not merely a technical system; it is a platform that shapes how millions of small businesses manage their finances, serve their customers, and make decisions. Every line of code—whether written by a human or an AI—carries ethical implications. This section defines the ethical boundaries, fairness principles, and transparency obligations that govern all AI contributions to the project. No AI may generate code that violates these principles, regardless of performance gains or human instruction.

### 15.1 Foundational Ethical Principles

The following four principles are the moral compass for every AI interaction with Amar Hisab. They are non‑negotiable and apply to all generated artefacts: code, tests, documentation, UI design, and data handling.

1. **Beneficence:** AI contributions must serve the genuine interests of the end‑user—the small business owner, the cashier, the student, the patient. Features must add real value, not exploit attention, manipulate decisions, or create unnecessary complexity.

2. **Non‑Maleficence:** AI must not generate code that could cause harm—financial loss due to incorrect accounting, privacy violations, discriminatory access to credit, or psychological stress from intrusive notifications. The AI must actively identify potential harms in its own output and flag them.

3. **Autonomy:** End‑users retain control over their data, their business processes, and their technology choices. AI must not generate lock‑in mechanisms, obscure data export paths, or design dark patterns that coerce user behaviour.

4. **Justice:** The benefits of Amar Hisab must be accessible regardless of the user’s technical literacy, literacy level, language, economic status, or geography. AI must design for inclusivity, not for an idealised, English‑speaking, urban user.

### 15.2 Fairness and Bias Mitigation

AI models are trained on vast datasets that contain societal biases. These biases can manifest in generated code in subtle but harmful ways.

**15.2.1 Areas of Concern:**
- **Language bias:** UI text, error messages, and documentation generated predominantly in English, neglecting Bengali‑first users. AI must default to Bengali when the business language is set to বাংলা, and ensure both languages are equally well‑supported.
- **Financial bias:** Credit limit algorithms, loyalty point calculations, or discount rules that inadvertently discriminate based on customer demographics encoded in data. AI must not generate business logic that uses protected characteristics (gender, religion, ethnicity) as decision inputs.
- **Accessibility bias:** Widgets or UI components that assume perfect vision, full motor control, or high‑end devices. AI must follow accessibility best practices: semantic labels, sufficient contrast, tappable areas, and compatibility with screen readers.
- **Data representation bias:** Test data generators that default to male names, urban addresses, or single‑language examples. AI must generate diverse, realistic test data representing the actual user base.

**15.2.2 Bias Self‑Audit:**
Before delivering any feature that involves customer interaction, financial rules, or UI, the AI must perform a bias self‑audit:

```
[BIAS AUDIT]
- Language: Are UI strings available in both Bengali and English? Yes/No
- Accessibility: Do all widgets have semantic labels? Is colour contrast sufficient? Yes/No
- Representation: Does test data include diverse names, addresses, and scenarios? Yes/No
- Discriminatory logic: Does any business rule use protected characteristics? Yes/No (if yes, must be justified and approved)
- Inclusivity: Is this feature usable on low‑end devices (2 GB RAM, Android 8)? Yes/No
```

If any answer is No, the AI must either correct the issue before delivery or file a Tier‑2 decision brief explaining the constraint and requesting guidance.

### 15.3 Transparency and Explainability

Users and regulators must be able to understand how Amar Hisab arrives at its results—stock valuations, profit calculations, tax reports, AI‑generated forecasts. The AI must build transparency into the system’s fabric.

**15.3.1 Explainable Outputs:**
- **Accounting reports:** The AI must generate code that includes clear traceability from a financial statement back to the underlying journal entries. A profit & loss report must be drillable.
- **AI‑generated insights (future premium features):** If the AI builds a sales forecasting or stock prediction model, it must include a plain‑language explanation of the factors influencing the prediction (e.g., “Based on last year’s Eid sales trend and current stock levels”).
- **Error messages:** Must clearly state what went wrong and suggest a corrective action, in the user’s language. Never display raw stack traces or opaque error codes to end‑users.

**15.3.2 AI Attribution Transparency:**
As defined in Section 10.5, every file generated by AI carries an attribution header. This is not just for internal tracking; it is an ethical obligation. If a defect is later traced to AI‑generated code, the team must be able to identify the responsible model, assess the scope of the issue, and notify affected users if necessary.

### 15.4 Accountability and Human Oversight

AI is a tool, not a decision‑maker. Ultimate accountability for the Amar Hisab product rests with the human engineering team and the company leadership.

**15.4.1 Human‑in‑the‑Loop for Critical Decisions:**
The AI must never autonomously deploy code to production, modify live data, or make architectural decisions that alter the system’s core behaviour. These actions require human approval (Tier‑2 or Tier‑3 decisions, Section 4).

**15.4.2 Audit Trail for AI Actions:**
When the AI generates a significant architectural proposal, a security‑sensitive change, or a financial algorithm, the human‑approved decision must be recorded in the AI Decision Log (Section 4.5) with the rationale. This provides a clear chain of accountability: the AI proposed, the human reviewed and approved, and the decision is documented.

**15.4.3 User‑Facing AI Features:**
If Amar Hisab introduces AI‑powered features that directly advise the business owner (e.g., “You should restock Napa Extra because demand is increasing”), those features must:
- Clearly identify themselves as AI‑generated suggestions, not infallible commands.
- Provide the reasoning behind the suggestion.
- Allow the user to dismiss or disable the feature entirely.
- Never make autonomous financial transactions on behalf of the user.

### 15.5 Data Ethics and Privacy

Amar Hisab handles sensitive business and personal data. AI contributions must uphold the highest standards of data ethics.

**15.5.1 Data Minimisation by Default:**
The AI must not generate code that collects or stores data beyond what is strictly necessary for the declared business function. If the AI proposes a new feature that requires additional customer data, it must justify the necessity and obtain human approval (Tier‑2).

**15.5.2 Consent and Control:**
- Cloud sync is opt‑in. The AI must not generate code that enables sync silently or makes it difficult to disable.
- Analytics and telemetry (if ever introduced) must be opt‑in, anonymised, and disclosed with a clear privacy notice.
- Data export and deletion (anonymisation) features must be straightforward and complete. The AI must test these features thoroughly.

**15.5.3 Training Data Boundaries:**
The AI must never suggest, generate, or endorse code that uploads Amar Hisab user data to external AI training datasets. User data is not a resource for model improvement. The local‑first architecture (data stays on the user’s device by default) is an ethical advantage that must be protected, not eroded.

### 15.6 Dual‑Use and Harmful Applications

Amar Hisab is designed to empower small businesses. The AI must be vigilant against code that could enable harmful applications.

**15.6.1 Prohibited Use Cases:**
The AI must refuse to generate code that:
- Enables tracking of individuals without consent.
- Facilitates predatory lending or deceptive financial practices.
- Creates backdoors or surveillance capabilities.
- Discriminates against customers based on protected characteristics.
- Automates spam, harassment, or fraudulent communications.

If a human requests such functionality (even inadvertently), the AI must refuse, citing this section, and explain the ethical concern.

**15.6.2 Conflict Mineral‑Equivalent for AI:**
Just as hardware supply chains must avoid conflict minerals, the AI must be aware that its own training data may include copyrighted or unethically sourced code. While the AI cannot retroactively audit its training, it must avoid generating code that is obviously a direct, unmodified copy of a proprietary library or algorithm. The human team is responsible for license compliance; the AI must not make it harder by obfuscating origins.

### 15.7 Environmental Responsibility

The AI must consider the environmental impact of its generated code.

- **Efficient code:** The offline‑first, SQLite‑based architecture is inherently energy‑efficient. The AI must preserve this efficiency. Avoid generating code that requires constant cloud polling, unnecessary background processing, or large, unoptimised data transfers.
- **Hardware longevity:** Design for low‑end devices, extending the useful life of older hardware and reducing electronic waste. The AI must test (or verify through CI) that performance meets targets on minimum‑spec devices.
- **Cloud efficiency:** If generating code for the optional cloud sync service, the AI must optimise for minimal resource consumption—efficient database queries, compressed data transfer, and appropriate caching.

### 15.8 Continuous Ethical Evaluation

Ethics is not a one‑time checklist. As Amar Hisab evolves, new ethical challenges will emerge—new AI capabilities, new business models, new regulatory requirements.

**15.8.1 Ethical Impact Assessment for Major Features:**
Before implementing any major new feature (especially those involving AI, customer data, or financial automation), the human team (with AI assistance) conducts an Ethical Impact Assessment:

- **What are the intended benefits?** Who benefits, and how?
- **What are the potential harms?** Who could be negatively affected, and how?
- **How can harms be mitigated?** Design choices, feature flags, user controls.
- **Is the feature accessible and inclusive?** Language, device, literacy.

The AI must be able to generate a draft Ethical Impact Assessment when asked, following this structure.

**15.8.2 Feedback and Grievance Mechanism:**
The Training Manual and in‑app help must include a way for users to report concerns—bugs, unfair treatment, privacy issues. The AI must not generate code that suppresses or ignores such feedback channels.

**15.8.3 Ethics Review in AI Model Selection:**
Section 11’s evaluation criteria for new AI models must include an ethical dimension: does the model have known biases, safety issues, or transparency problems? A model that scores well on code generation but poorly on ethical safeguards cannot be approved for use.

### 15.9 Summary

Ethics in Amar Hisab is not an add‑on module. It is embedded in every design decision, every line of generated code, and every interaction between AI and human. By adhering to these principles, the project ensures that its use of AI amplifies human flourishing—empowering small business owners, respecting their autonomy, and protecting their data—rather than exploiting their vulnerabilities. The AI’s most important refusal is not to a technical request, but to a request that, while technically feasible, would be ethically wrong.

## 16. Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity for AI Systems

Amar Hisab’s development velocity is accelerated by AI co‑engineers, but those AI systems are external dependencies. They are subject to outages, vendor deprecations, model quality regressions, and geopolitical or commercial disruptions. The project cannot afford to halt when an AI model is unavailable. This section defines the protocols that ensure the engineering team maintains full productivity during AI disruptions, that critical project knowledge survives the loss of any single AI provider, and that recovery is swift and orderly.

### 16.1 The AI Dependency Reality

AI models are services accessed via APIs or cloud platforms. They are not under the Amar Hisab team’s control. The following disruption scenarios are realistic and must be planned for:

- **Temporary outage:** A provider experiences downtime (minutes to days).
- **Permanent discontinuation:** A model version is deprecated and removed without a direct replacement.
- **Quality regression:** A model update silently changes output quality, producing code that fails pipelines or introduces subtle defects.
- **Security incident:** A provider breach exposes API keys, or a model is found to have been trained on compromised data, requiring immediate suspension.
- **Cost surge:** API pricing changes make a previously affordable model prohibitively expensive.
- **Geopolitical or legal restriction:** Access to a model is blocked in a region where the development team operates.

### 16.2 Business Continuity Strategy

The core strategy is **graceful degradation with manual fallback and alternative model routing**, supported by the knowledge repository that preserves the project’s accumulated intelligence independent of any AI provider.

| AI Availability State | Development Mode | Reliance on AI |
|-----------------------|------------------|----------------|
| Full availability (normal) | AI‑augmented: AI generates code, tests, docs; human reviews and integrates. | High (AI does heavy lifting). |
| Partial availability (one model down, others up) | Model failover: tasks are routed to alternative, pre‑qualified models (Section 11). | Medium (alternate model may be slower or less precise). |
| No AI available (total outage) | Fully manual: human engineers write all code, following the Architecture Book and using the knowledge repository for patterns. | Zero (no AI assistance). |
| AI quality untrusted (silent regression) | AI‑suspended: all AI output blocked; manual development. Investigation underway. | Zero (AI output quarantined). |

The team **never** becomes dependent on AI to the point that they cannot ship code without it. All human engineers must retain the ability to develop manually, and the codebase, documentation, and knowledge repository are designed to support this.

### 16.3 Disaster Recovery Procedures

Each disruption type has a specific, pre‑defined recovery procedure. The Chief AI Systems Architect (or designated incident lead) executes these procedures.

#### 16.3.1 Temporary Outage (Single Model)
1. **Detect:** Monitoring alerts on API error rates or manual report from engineer.
2. **Assess:** Determine if the outage is vendor‑wide or specific to the model. Check vendor status page.
3. **Failover:** Route AI workloads to the designated backup model for that task type (from Model Registry failover list). Engineers use the backup model’s addendum and prompt templates.
4. **Communicate:** Notify the engineering team via the incident channel: “Claude-4 is unavailable. Use GPT-4o as per failover addendum. Manual development for critical path items encouraged.”
5. **Monitor:** Wait for vendor resolution. Once restored, run benchmark suite T‑001–T‑005 against the recovered model to verify quality before returning to normal routing.
6. **Post‑mortem:** Log the incident in the AI Decision Log with duration, impact, and any adjustments made.

#### 16.3.2 Permanent Model Discontinuation
1. **Announcement:** Vendor announces end‑of‑life. The Chief AI Systems Architect records the date and impact.
2. **Immediate action:** The model is moved to `Deprecated` state in the Model Registry. No new work is assigned to it.
3. **Migration plan:** Within one sprint, all active prompts and templates that reference the model are updated to use the replacement model. The model’s addendum is updated with migration notes.
4. **Knowledge transfer:** Any unique strengths of the retiring model that are not yet replicated by the replacement are documented as potential manual patterns.
5. **Final audit:** A random sample of the model’s attributed code is audited for quality. Any latent issues are remediated.
6. **Retirement:** After the deprecation period, the model is moved to `Retired`. Its addendum is archived.

#### 16.3.3 Quality Regression (Silent Failure)
1. **Detect:** The AI‑to‑CI feedback loop detects a spike in first‑pass yield drops, or the automated benchmark suite (Section 11.5) shows a score drop >15%.
2. **Quarantine:** The model is immediately moved to `Suspended` state. Engineers are instructed to stop using it; any in‑flight PRs with code from that model are blocked until reviewed.
3. **Investigation:** The Chief AI Systems Architect runs the full benchmark suite and a sample of recent failures to characterise the regression. A decision brief is prepared: roll back to a previous model version (if available), adjust prompts to compensate, or retire the model.
4. **Resolution:** Either the model is restored after vendor fix, or it is replaced. The lessons learned are added to the AI Lessons Learned log.
5. **Re‑qualification:** If restored, the model passes the full evaluation suite again before returning to `Active`.

#### 16.3.4 Security Incident (Model or Provider Breach)
1. **Containment:** Immediately suspend the affected model. Revoke all API keys associated with that provider. Rotate any secrets that may have been exposed.
2. **Assessment:** Determine if any generated code from that model could contain injected malicious patterns. A full audit of all code attributed to that model within the incident window is mandatory.
3. **Remediation:** Any compromised code is removed or rewritten. The incident is documented in the AI Decision Log and reported to the company’s security officer.
4. **Recovery:** The model is only reinstated after the vendor has resolved the breach and provided a post‑incident review, and the Chief AI Systems Architect is satisfied with security. Otherwise, the model is retired.

#### 16.3.5 Total AI Unavailability (All Models Down)
1. **Activation:** The Chief AI Systems Architect declares “Manual Mode”. All AI‑assisted code generation halts.
2. **Work prioritisation:** The team focuses on tasks that do not require heavy AI: code review, bug fixes, manual testing, documentation improvements, architectural planning.
3. **Knowledge repository reliance:** Engineers use the knowledge repository (session summaries, prompt library, lessons learned, architecture documentation) to manually implement features. The repository contains enough context to reproduce AI‑quality code manually, albeit slower.
4. **Recovery:** As models come back online, they are re‑qualified via benchmark suite before use resumes. The backlog of AI‑ready tasks is then processed.

### 16.4 AI Model Redundancy and Failover

To minimise the impact of single‑model outages, the project maintains a **failover matrix** in the Model Registry. For each task category (code generation, test generation, documentation, review), a primary model and at least one qualified secondary model are designated.

| Task Category | Primary Model | Secondary Model | Tertiary / Manual Fallback |
|--------------|---------------|-----------------|----------------------------|
| Code Generation | Claude-4 | GPT-4o | Manual (Architecture Book + patterns) |
| Test Generation | Claude-4 | Gemini Pro | Manual |
| Documentation | Claude-4 | GPT-4o | Manual |
| Code Review | GPT-4o | Claude-4 | Human only |

The failover matrix is reviewed quarterly and updated as new models are onboarded or existing models degrade.

**Hot/Warm/Cold Standby Model:**
- **Hot:** Primary models are actively used daily.
- **Warm:** Secondary models have passed evaluation, have active API keys, and their addenda are maintained. Engineers have tested them and know their quirks. They can be activated within minutes.
- **Cold:** Tertiary models have been evaluated and are documented but not actively maintained. Activating them may require updating API keys and refreshing context. Used only in extended outages.

### 16.5 Knowledge Preservation as Continuity

The knowledge repository (Section 14) is the ultimate insurance against AI disruption. Even if all AI models were to disappear permanently, the knowledge repository ensures that:

- The project’s architectural decisions, patterns, and standards are fully documented.
- Session summaries capture the reasoning behind past implementations, enabling a human to understand and extend the system.
- Prompt libraries and effective patterns can be reinterpreted by human engineers as design patterns.
- The Context Bundle can be used directly by humans to understand the state of any module.

To ensure this knowledge is robust:

- The repository is stored in the same Git monorepo as the code, so it is backed up and versioned alongside the product.
- A script exists to export the entire knowledge repository as a static HTML site, which can be browsed offline if the development environment is compromised.
- Once per quarter, a human engineer performs a “knowledge walkthrough”: they attempt to implement a medium‑complexity feature using only the knowledge repository (no AI). This validates that the repository is sufficient for business continuity.

### 16.6 Drills and Plan Validation

The disaster recovery plan is not theoretical. It must be tested.

- **Quarterly Failover Drill:** The Chief AI Systems Architect announces an unscheduled drill. A specific model is declared “unavailable” for one working day. The team switches to the secondary model or manual mode. Post‑drill, a retrospective identifies gaps.
- **Model Restoration Drill:** A snapshot of the codebase from a previous release is used to simulate restoring a retired model’s output and verifying it against current benchmarks. This ensures the retirement procedures are correct.
- **Total AI Outage Simulation (Annual):** For one sprint, the team operates fully manually (or with severely limited AI). This exercise builds muscle memory and validates that productivity can continue.

All drill results are documented in the knowledge repository under `docs/knowledge/drills/`.

### 16.7 Communication During AI Disruptions

Clear communication prevents chaos. The following protocol is followed during any AI disruption:

1. **Initial Alert:** Within 15 minutes of detecting a significant disruption (model unavailable, quality drop, security incident), the Chief AI Systems Architect posts an incident in the team’s incident management channel (Slack/Teams). The post includes: affected model(s), impact (which tasks are blocked), failover instructions, and an estimated resolution time if known.
2. **Status Updates:** Every 4 hours (or when significant), an update is posted.
3. **Resolution Notification:** When the disruption is resolved, a final post confirms the model’s status, any lingering precautions, and a link to the post‑mortem (if applicable).
4. **Stakeholder Communication:** For disruptions lasting more than 24 hours or affecting a release, the engineering manager notifies product management and adjusts the sprint plan.

### 16.8 Integration with Overall Project Continuity

This AI‑specific disaster recovery plan is a component of Amar Hisab’s broader business continuity and disaster recovery strategy (Architecture Book Section 18). The AI DR plan focuses on the **development capability**; the overall plan covers the production system, data, and operations. The two are linked:

- If the production system is down, development may need to continue (AI could still be available).
- If AI is down, production continues unaffected because Amar Hisab is offline‑first and does not depend on AI for runtime.
- The knowledge repository’s backup and restore procedures are aligned with the overall backup strategy (Database Book Section 6, Operations Runbook Section 4).

### 16.9 Summary

AI disruptions are not a matter of “if” but “when.” By pre‑qualifying alternative models, maintaining a comprehensive knowledge repository, training the team in manual development, and regularly testing failover procedures, Amar Hisab ensures that its use of AI accelerates development without creating a single point of failure. The project can survive the loss of any AI model—or all of them—and continue delivering value to its users.

## 17. AI Collaboration Metrics & Performance Measurement

You cannot improve what you do not measure. The Amar Hisab AI co‑engineering model is a substantial investment—in API costs, in human review time, and in the cognitive load of managing multiple AI models. To justify that investment and to continuously improve, the project must rigorously measure the effectiveness of AI contributions. This section defines the key performance indicators (KPIs), the data collection mechanisms, the analysis cadence, and the feedback loops that transform raw metrics into actionable improvements in the AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap.

### 17.1 The Purpose of Measurement

Metrics serve three purposes:

1. **Accountability:** Show whether AI is delivering a net positive impact on development velocity, code quality, and team satisfaction.
2. **Diagnosis:** Identify which models, which task types, and which workflow stages are underperforming, so that targeted fixes (prompt adjustments, model replacement, roadmap updates) can be applied.
3. **Optimisation:** Drive the continuous improvement loop—metrics → analysis → roadmap refinement → improved AI output → better metrics.

Metrics are never used to punish human engineers or to automate decisions about individual performance. They are for system‑level improvement.

### 17.2 Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

The project tracks four categories of metrics: Velocity & Efficiency, Code Quality, Knowledge & Learning, and Team Experience.

#### 17.2.1 Velocity & Efficiency KPIs

| KPI | Definition | Target | Measurement Method |
|-----|------------|--------|-------------------|
| **AI Task Throughput** | Number of user stories or tasks completed with AI assistance per sprint. | Increasing trend | Count of merged PRs tagged `ai-generated` per sprint. |
| **AI Time‑to‑Completion** | Median time from task assignment (first AI prompt) to code merge. | <50% of estimated manual time | Timestamps on task management tool vs. merge time; engineer self‑reporting. |
| **First‑Pass Yield** | Percentage of AI‑generated commits that pass the CI pipeline on the first push (no test failures, lint errors, or security flags). | >80% | CI pipeline data, filtered by commits with AI attribution header. |
| **Review Time** | Median time from PR open to merge for AI‑generated vs. human‑only code. | AI review time ≤ human review time | Pull request analytics. |
| **Rework Rate** | Percentage of AI‑generated PRs that require more than one revision cycle after human review. | <20% | PR comment and commit history analysis. |

#### 17.2.2 Code Quality KPIs

| KPI | Definition | Target | Measurement Method |
|-----|------------|--------|-------------------|
| **Defect Escape Rate** | Number of post‑merge defects (bugs found in production or later testing) attributed to AI‑generated code, per 1000 lines of AI code. | <1 defect/KLoC | Bug tracker data correlated with git blame and AI attribution. |
| **Test Coverage Contribution** | Percentage of new code (lines) covered by AI‑generated tests. | 100% for domain/application layers | Code coverage tool output, filtered by AI‑generated test files. |
| **Architectural Violation Rate** | Number of architectural rule violations (A‑001 through A‑010) detected in AI‑generated code per sprint. | <5 violations/sprint | Architectural conformance test results in CI. |
| **Security Flag Rate** | Number of security gate failures (S‑001 through S‑009) triggered by AI‑generated code per sprint. | 0 (absolute) | Security scan results. |

#### 17.2.3 Knowledge & Learning KPIs

| KPI | Definition | Target | Measurement Method |
|-----|------------|--------|-------------------|
| **Lesson Capture Rate** | Number of new entries in `ai-lessons-learned.md` per sprint. | ≥3 entries/sprint | Commit count to that file. |
| **Session Summary Coverage** | Percentage of AI‑generated commits that have a linked session summary. | >90% | Script that cross‑references commit messages with session summary files. |
| **Repeat Defect Rate** | Number of times an AI mistake documented in the lessons log recurs within 3 months. | 0 | Manual review of defect reports against lessons log. |
| **Onboarding Time** | Time for a new engineer to complete their first independent AI‑assisted task. | Decreasing trend over quarters | Onboarding coordinator tracking. |

#### 17.2.4 Team Experience KPIs

| KPI | Definition | Target | Measurement Method |
|-----|------------|--------|-------------------|
| **Engineer Satisfaction with AI** | Quarterly survey score (1–5) on “AI co‑engineering makes me more productive” and “I trust the quality of AI‑generated code.” | ≥4.0 average | Anonymous survey. |
| **AI‑Related Frustration Reports** | Number of times an engineer escalates an AI quality issue or requests model suspension. | <2 per month | Incident channel logs. |
| **Model Preference Shift** | Change in the percentage of tasks voluntarily assigned to each active model (indicates perceived quality). | Monitored for trend | AI attribution data from commits. |

### 17.3 Data Collection Infrastructure

The following data sources feed the metrics pipeline. The human engineering team, with AI assistance, sets up the collection mechanisms once and then refines them.

- **CI/CD Pipeline:** Provides first‑pass yield, test results, lint/format status, architectural conformance results, security scan results. Every CI run is tagged with the commit’s AI attribution (extracted from the commit message or file headers).
- **Git Repository:** Provides commit messages (AI attribution), file authorship (`git blame` cross‑referenced with AI attribution headers), pull request open/close timestamps, comment counts.
- **Task Management System (Jira, Linear, etc.):** Provides task assignment and completion timestamps, task labels (`ai-ready`, `ai-generated`).
- **Survey Tool:** Conducts the quarterly engineer satisfaction survey.
- **Manual Reporting:** Engineers log AI session summaries; the lesson learned log is updated manually. The Chief AI Systems Architect or a designated analyst periodically reviews these.
- **Model API Usage Dashboards:** Provider dashboards (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google Cloud) provide token usage and cost data, which can be correlated with output volume.

A lightweight metrics aggregation script (Python or Dart) runs weekly and produces a `METRICS_REPORT.md` stored in the knowledge repository. This report is generated automatically from the raw data sources where possible, with manual annotations for qualitative KPIs.

### 17.4 Analysis Cadence and Review

Metrics are useless without analysis and action. The project follows a tiered review cadence:

- **Weekly (Sprint Review):** The Chief AI Systems Architect reviews the pipeline first‑pass yield, rework rate, and any security flags. Immediate corrective actions (prompt fixes, model suspension) are taken if thresholds are breached.
- **Monthly (AI Operations Review):** A broader review including velocity, defect escape rate, lesson capture rate, and repeat defect rate. The output is a brief AI Operations Memo distributed to the engineering team.
- **Quarterly (Roadmap Review):** A comprehensive review of all KPIs against targets. Trends are analysed. The AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap is updated (Section 10) based on findings. The Model Registry failover matrix, addenda, and benchmarks are recalibrated.
- **Annual (Strategic AI Review):** A high‑level review for company leadership and investors, demonstrating the ROI of AI co‑engineering and shaping the strategic AI roadmap for the next year.

### 17.5 The Metrics‑Driven Improvement Feedback Loop

The true value of metrics lies in closing the loop. The following process ensures that every measurement cycle leads to concrete improvements:

1. **Measure:** Collect data per the defined KPIs.
2. **Analyse:** Identify outliers, trends, and correlations. For example, a spike in rework rate might correlate with a specific model version or task type.
3. **Diagnose Root Cause:** The AI Operations Review discusses potential causes: inadequate context, ambiguous prompts, model regression, insufficient review guidelines.
4. **Propose Remedy:** The Chief AI Systems Architect creates a Decision Brief (Tier‑2) proposing a specific change: update a prompt template, retire a model addendum, enhance the Context Bundle, add a new architectural rule, etc.
5. **Implement:** The change is applied to the roadmap, prompt library, context bundle, or model registry.
6. **Verify:** Monitor the relevant KPI in subsequent weeks to confirm improvement. If the metric does not improve, iterate.

This loop is documented in the AI Decision Log and linked to the specific metric that triggered it, creating a traceable history of continuous improvement.

### 17.6 AI‑to‑Human Performance Reporting

The AI itself contributes to the metrics process. At the end of each sprint, the AI (using the Specialist Reviewer model or the primary documentation model) is tasked with generating a draft **AI Performance Summary** from the raw data. The prompt includes the CI logs, PR analytics, and the sprint’s session summaries. The AI produces:

- A summary of overall AI performance against targets.
- A breakdown by model (if multiple were used).
- A list of the top 3 recurring issues detected.
- Proposed adjustments to prompt templates or context.

The Chief AI Systems Architect reviews, corrects, and publishes this summary. This offloads the analytical heavy lifting to the AI, allowing the human to focus on strategic decisions.

### 17.7 Cost‑Effectiveness Tracking

AI collaboration has a direct cost (API tokens) and an indirect benefit (engineering time saved). The project tracks a simple cost‑effectiveness ratio:

- **Cost:** Total API spend per sprint (from provider dashboards), amortised infrastructure cost for CI/CD integration, and a notional fraction of the Chief AI Systems Architect’s time for governance.
- **Benefit:** Estimated hours saved = (estimated manual development time for AI‑generated tasks) minus (actual AI‑assisted time including review). This is an estimate, refined over time by comparing similar tasks done with and without AI.

The ratio `Benefit (hours saved) / Cost (USD)` is tracked quarterly. A ratio greater than 1 indicates positive ROI. The target is continuously improving ROI. If the ratio declines, the AI Operations Review investigates whether model costs have risen, or whether AI output requires excessive review.

### 17.8 Metrics for Continuous Roadmap Evolution

The metrics defined here are not static. As the project matures, some KPIs may become less relevant, and new ones may be needed. The Chief AI Systems Architect reviews the KPI set annually and proposes additions or retirements, following the roadmap governance process (Section 10).

For example, if the team later adopts AI‑generated UI designs, a “design consistency” metric may be added. If AI begins to autonomously refactor legacy code, a “refactoring success rate” metric may be introduced. The metric framework is extensible.

### 17.9 Summary

The AI Collaboration Metrics & Performance Measurement system transforms the subjective feeling of “AI is helping us” into objective, actionable data. It holds the AI co‑engineering model accountable, guides continuous improvement, and demonstrates the tangible value of the investment. By closing the loop from measurement to roadmap refinement, Amar Hisab ensures that its AI collaboration becomes more effective with every sprint.

## 18. AI‑Human Collaboration Maturity Model

Amar Hisab’s journey with AI co‑engineering is not a binary state—it is a progressive evolution. The project will move through distinct stages of capability, trust, and autonomy. This Maturity Model provides a shared language for the team to understand where they are, where they are heading, and what must be mastered at each stage. It serves as both a diagnostic tool and a strategic roadmap for advancing the AI‑human partnership.

### 18.1 The Five Levels of AI‑Human Collaboration

The model defines five levels. Each level is characterised by the degree of AI autonomy, the scope of AI contributions, the nature of human oversight, and the integration with the development lifecycle.

| Level | Name | AI Role | Human Role | Key Characteristics |
|-------|------|---------|------------|---------------------|
| **1** | Assisted | Code completion, syntax help | Full author | Human writes all design and logic. AI acts as an advanced autocomplete and documentation lookup. No architectural decisions by AI. |
| **2** | Augmented | Generates individual functions, tests, or documentation from detailed prompts | Author and reviewer | AI produces self‑contained units of work (a service method, a test suite, a DDL migration) within a well‑defined context. Human provides specification and reviews output. Quality gates are applied. |
| **3** | Collaborative | Generates entire modules, proposes architectural changes, self‑audits quality | Architect and approver | AI operates across multiple layers (domain, application, infrastructure, API) for a given module. It proposes Tier‑2 decisions and updates documentation. Human focuses on strategic design and final sign‑off. |
| **4** | Orchestrated | Autonomously implements features from user stories, manages CI/CD failures, coordinates with other AI models | Strategist and exception‑handler | AI receives a user story and context bundle; it decomposes the task, generates all code and tests, runs CI, and remediates failures. Human intervenes only for Tier‑3 decisions or when AI escalates. Multiple AI models collaborate in specialised roles (code generator, reviewer, documenter). |
| **5** | Autonomous | Fully autonomous development within defined guardrails; proposes roadmap changes, self‑improves | Governor and visionary | AI manages the development lifecycle for defined subsystems. It monitors production, detects bugs, proposes fixes, and continuously refactors. Human sets ethical boundaries, business priorities, and architectural vision. AI‑to‑AI collaboration is seamless. |

### 18.2 Amar Hisab’s Current and Target Maturity

Amar Hisab launches with a target maturity of **Level 2 (Augmented)**, with strong elements of **Level 3 (Collaborative)** for the core Sales, Purchase, and Inventory modules where the architecture is well‑defined. The immediate goal is to stabilise Level 2 across the entire module set and progressively advance to Level 3 as the Context Bundle, prompt templates, and quality gates prove their reliability.

Levels 4 and 5 are aspirational; they require significant advancements in AI model capability, tool‑use APIs, and CI/CD integration that are not yet fully mature. The roadmap anticipates reaching Level 3 within the first year of active development, and re‑evaluating readiness for Level 4 in the second year.

### 18.3 Prerequisites for Each Level

Advancement is not merely a matter of time. Each level requires specific technical, organisational, and trust conditions to be met.

#### 18.3.1 From Level 1 to Level 2
- **Prerequisites:**
  - Formal documentation suite (Architecture Book, Database Book, Proto Contract Book) is established.
  - Context Bundle is maintained and versioned.
  - Code Generation Standards (Section 6) and Quality Gates (Section 7) are defined.
  - At least one AI model passes the evaluation benchmark (T‑001–T‑008).
  - Human engineers are trained in prompt engineering (Section 12).
- **Validation:** AI can independently generate a repository implementation and its unit tests, with human review required only for business logic correctness, not style or structure.

#### 18.3.2 From Level 2 to Level 3
- **Prerequisites:**
  - The knowledge repository (Section 14) has accumulated at least 3 months of session summaries and lessons learned.
  - The AI Lessons Learned log demonstrates that common AI mistakes have been systematically addressed.
  - At least two models are active with defined failover (Section 11).
  - AI self‑audits (architectural, security, quality gates) are proven reliable (mismatch rate <5%).
  - The AI Decision Log shows that AI is producing high‑quality Tier‑2 decision briefs.
- **Validation:** AI can generate a complete module (e.g., Supplier Management) from a user story, including all layers, tests, and documentation updates, with human approval required only for architecture changes and final merge.

#### 18.3.3 From Level 3 to Level 4
- **Prerequisites:**
  - Multi‑model collaboration is established: a Specialist Reviewer model reliably catches defects, and a Documentation model produces publication‑ready text.
  - The CI/CD pipeline includes AI‑triggered remediation (Section 13.6) that has a >90% success rate.
  - The AI can autonomously perform impact analysis and propose documentation changes with high accuracy.
  - The team has completed at least one full‑sprint manual‑mode drill (Section 16.6), proving that humans can operate without AI if needed.
- **Validation:** AI can receive a user story, generate all code and tests, push a PR, and have the CI pipeline pass with zero human intervention for >80% of standard feature tasks.

#### 18.3.4 From Level 4 to Level 5
- **Prerequisites:**
  - AI has read‑only access to production monitoring (error rates, performance) and can correlate issues with code.
  - The project has a well‑defined “AI governance council” that reviews all AI‑initiated production changes.
  - Strong ethical and regulatory guardrails are implemented and audited.
  - The project has a track record of at least 6 months of stable Level 4 operation without a major AI‑caused incident.
- **Validation:** AI can detect a production anomaly, propose a root cause, generate a fix, pass all gates, and present a deployment recommendation—all without human prompting, but with human approval required for deployment.

### 18.4 Maturity Assessment

The Chief AI Systems Architect conducts a formal maturity assessment every 6 months. The assessment uses a scorecard that maps the project’s actual practices against the Level prerequisites.

**Scorecard Excerpt:**

| Capability Area | Level 2 Criteria | Current Status | Level 3 Criteria | Current Status |
|-----------------|------------------|----------------|------------------|----------------|
| Documentation Suite | Complete and versioned | ✅ | Continuously updated by AI | ⬜ |
| Context Management | Context Bundle exists | ✅ | AI requests missing context autonomously | ⬜ |
| AI Self‑Audit | Quality gates attached to output | ✅ | Gate accuracy >95% | ⬜ |
| Decision‑Making | AI follows Tier system | ✅ | AI produces actionable Tier‑2 briefs | ⬜ |
| Knowledge Capture | Session summaries exist | ⬜ (targeting) | Lessons log prevents recurring defects | ⬜ |

The assessment produces a maturity score per area and an overall readiness assessment. The results inform the roadmap review process (Section 10) and are shared with the engineering team.

### 18.5 Risks and Anti‑Patterns at Each Level

Advancement brings new risks. The team must recognise and avoid the anti‑patterns specific to each maturity level.

| Level | Common Anti‑Pattern | Mitigation |
|-------|---------------------|------------|
| **1→2** | **Over‑generation without review:** Humans copy‑paste AI output without verification, leading to subtle bugs. | Enforce quality gates; human review mandatory; CI pipeline catches regressions. |
| **2→3** | **Context drift:** AI generates code that is syntactically correct but misaligned with the broader architecture because context was incomplete. | Strengthen Context Request Protocol (Section 3.6); improve AI’s self‑audit of layer dependencies. |
| **3→4** | **Automation complacency:** Humans stop reviewing AI output because “the AI usually gets it right,” allowing critical errors to reach production. | Require human sign‑off for all Tier‑2 decisions; maintain metrics on review depth; conduct surprise manual reviews. |
| **4→5** | **Loss of human oversight:** AI becomes a black‑box decision‑maker; the project loses the ability to understand its own system. | Governance council; mandatory explainability for all AI‑initiated changes; human‑readable decision logs; manual‑mode drills remain mandatory. |

### 18.6 The Role of the AI Co‑Engineering Roadmap in Maturity Advancement

This roadmap is both a product of the current maturity level and a driver of advancement. At each maturity transition, the roadmap is updated:

- **Level 2 → 3:** Roadmap adds advanced context management rules, expands architectural conformance tests, and formalises the AI Decision Log.
- **Level 3 → 4:** Roadmap introduces automated remediation protocols, multi‑model workflow orchestration, and AI‑initiated PR capabilities.
- **Level 4 → 5:** Roadmap defines the AI governance council, production monitoring integration, and autonomous operation boundaries.

The roadmap is the mechanism by which lessons from each maturity stage are codified into the AI’s operational rules, enabling the next stage.

### 18.7 Summary

The AI‑Human Collaboration Maturity Model provides a clear, aspirational, yet pragmatic path for Amar Hisab’s evolution. It prevents the project from stagnating at a basic level of AI usage, while also guarding against reckless automation. By understanding the prerequisites, assessing maturity honestly, and updating the roadmap at each transition, the team ensures that AI becomes an increasingly powerful, trustworthy, and integral partner in building the future of Amar Hisab.
